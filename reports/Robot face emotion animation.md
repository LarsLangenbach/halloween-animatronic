# Borrowed robot science can bring Frank alive

Almost everything Frank's face engine needs has already been built and published, and most of it is old. Around 2000, MIT's Kismet treated a robot's feelings as a single point drifting across a small emotion map and computed the face as a weighted blend of a few hand-made anchor faces. Disney has since published how its animatronics stack an always-on "alive" layer of blinks, glances and breathing under triggered clips and live input. Psychologists have measured human blinks, gaze shifts and expression timing closely enough that the numbers can go straight into code. The new ingredient is the language model. Since Engineered Arts let GPT-3 pick Ameca's expressions in March 2023, the systems with published detail have had the model choose *what* to show, usually from a closed menu, while a local engine decides *how* and *when*. Apart from one hobby InMoov experiment, no system the researchers found lets an LLM write servo angles, and none drives a physical servo face from a continuous emotion state fed by LLM tags. Frank's design, with Claude emitting ten tags into a local engine that owns timing and limits, therefore follows the field's consensus split, and as far as the notes show, nobody has published this exact combination. The failures this field documents are mostly failures of timing and mismatch. On Ameca, expressions held back until after speech struck testers as "not correctly timed", and faces that contradict the words made people doubt themselves. The plan below follows from that. An orchestrator runs the nine controller states and fires each tag against the audio clock. A synthesiser keeps a slow mood and a mixture of feelings that rise and fade, and renders them one face region at a time with deliberate asymmetry. A 50 Hz animator layers blinks, gaze, speech and reflexes through smoothing springs and per-servo limits. The whole thing is a few hundred lines of Python, and `firmware/brow_anim.py`, `firmware/eye_anim.py` and `frank/mouth.py` already cover a good share of it.

## Twenty-five years of robot faces settled on one split: the AI picks, the engine performs

Researchers and companies have tried three ways to give a robot an emotional face. The oldest treats emotion as a position on a map and slides the face smoothly between a handful of hand-made anchor faces. The most common, used by nearly every commercial robot, gives animators a library of authored expressions and a playback engine that layers blinks, glances and lip movement on top. The newest lets the robot learn its own face by watching itself in a camera. All three divide the work the same way: something slow and high-level chooses the feeling, and a separate fast layer handles blending, timing and motor limits. Frank's character bible already asks for this split when it says the AI decides intent and the motor controller owns timing, blending and limits.

**Kismet is the direct ancestor of Frank's synthesiser.** MIT's page describes its expressions as "generated using an interpolation-based technique over a three dimensional space" whose axes are arousal, valence and stance. **Nine basis postures span that space**, and the current emotional state, a single moving point, sets how much each posture contributes to the face, in real time and along smooth trajectories ([MIT CSAIL: Kismet facial expressions](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/lbr/sociable/facial-expression.html)). Each prototype moves most features a little. The valence prototypes mainly curl the lips, for example, but they also move the ears, lids, brows and jaw. The same page notes that one face served four jobs at once: emotive expression, social displays, reflexes such as shutting the eyes against a threat, and synchronisation with speech. Frank's servos face the same competition. The journal write-up appeared in 2003 ([MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/emotion-and-sociable-humanoid-robots-2)), and Rodney Brooks dates the lab's eye-control work to "our 1995 implementations on Cog and Kismet" ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/disney-research-robotic-gaze)). Kismet's emotion-arbitration equations and decay constants are in the full 2003 paper, which the researchers could not retrieve, so those details remain unverified here. Two later heads kept the continuous idea. Waseda's WE-4RII (2004) used a 3-D "mental space" of pleasantness, activation and certainty, with mood and desires as separate slower states, on 26 facial degrees of freedom ([Takanishi, Humanoids 2004](https://archive.iar.kit.edu/workshops/humanoids2004/Takanishi.pdf)). Brussels' Probo (2010) placed its expressions as vectors in Russell's two-axis circumplex and mapped them onto 20 head DOF ([Saldien et al. 2010](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FS12369-010-0067-6)).

The library-plus-engine family is where the animation craft lives. Philips' iCat animation engine (IROS 2004) let the robot speak while tracking a user's head, lip-syncing, blinking and showing expressions, all at once ([indexed abstract](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2101506210)). A related patent spells out a lesson Frank should not relearn: starting a new animation from neutral "may lead to delays and therefore less-interactive behaviour", and the jump is abrupt when one animation ends somewhere other than where the next begins ([US 2007/0191986](https://uspto.report/patent/app/20070191986)). Several heads built their actuators around FACS, the system that codes the face by individual muscle actions. Wrocław's EMYS (2013) did so for six basic emotions ([Kędzierski et al.](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12369-013-0183-1.pdf)). RIKEN's Nikola (2022) placed 29 pneumatic face actuators by FACS and produced 17 action units correctly. Its silicone skin wrinkled poorly, though, so disgust was hard to show ([RIKEN](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2022/20220210_1/index.html)), and Frank's cast skin will hit the same limit.

**Disney has published the most usable runtime recipe.** Its 2020 gaze paper built on a 19-DOF Audio-Animatronics bust with neck, eyes, lids and brows. It stacks layers in which "higher levels are able to 'subsume' lower levels": L0 holds default motor positions, L1 "alive" runs breathing, blinks and saccades, L2 is a reading behaviour, L3 a glance toward a stimulus, L4 engagement with a person, and L5 an acknowledgement nod laid over the top. The payoff, in the authors' words: changing how fast the robot saccades, blinks or breathes "only involves changing a single behavioral layer" ([Pan et al. 2020, full text](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)). Each person in view gets a curiosity score built from motion and distance and damped by habituation, and the authors call the scoring weights a way for "character creators [to] shape the interactive personality". They describe their stance as "more animation-y than biology-y or psychology-y" ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/disney-research-robotic-gaze)). Disney's 2024 bipedal character runs a looped background animation with "intermittent eye-blinking", blends triggered clips over it in **0.1 s for show functions such as eyes and antennas and 0.35 s for the body**, and applies live joystick input last ([arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204)). The December 2025 Olaf paper drives eyes, eyelids, eyebrows and jaw with ordinary PD control because their low inertia barely affects the robot's dynamics ([arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705)). Frank's hobby servos sit in that same "show function" regime, so nothing in his face needs machine learning to move well.

Engineered Arts' Ameca follows the same pattern commercially. It has 27 DOF in the head and face, "over 50 realistic facial expressions" authored in its Tritium Poses and Animator tools, and autonomous passive "human behaviors" for eye contact, looking around, blinking and breathing ([Engineered Arts](https://engineeredarts.com/robots/ameca)). Anki built Cozmo with a Maya pipeline led by Pixar-trained animators ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024221/Cozmo-Animation-Pipeline-for-a)). A community study of Vector records design rules that suit Frank well: "Vector's emotions are stable. He does not rapidly cycle back and forth between two emotional states", behaviours carry individually crafted cool-down timers, an eye manager breaks eye contact before it lasts too long, and the body stays "slightly moving at all times" ([Vector character study](https://randym32.github.io/Anki.Vector.Documentation/guides/Vector%20character%20study.html)). The learned family comes from Columbia. Emo (2024) has 26 actuators under silicone and learned its own face from hours of random "babbling" in front of a camera. It predicts a human smile about **840 ms before it happens** and smiles along, because "delayed facial mimicry looks disingenuous" ([Columbia Engineering](https://engineering.columbia.edu/news/robot-can-you-say-cheese)). Its 2026 lip-sync follow-up still had "particular difficulties with hard sounds like 'B'" and with puckered sounds such as "W" ([Columbia Engineering](https://www.engineering.columbia.edu/about/news/robot-learns-lip-sync)). These systems need camera rigs and training runs, and Frank does not need them to perform well.

A 2022 survey of the field splits robot expression generation into hand-coded and automated approaches ([Rawal & Stock-Homburg](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07169)). Every research and commercial system the researchers found also splits the deciding from the doing. A puppeteer triggers Disney's clips while the engine composes them. GPT picks Ameca's expressions while Tritium plays them. Kismet's emotion system moves a point while the posture blend renders it. The decision layer is sparse and slow, about one decision per phrase. The execution layer is dense and fast and never waits for the decision layer.

## Language models arrived in 2023, and their failures are timing and mismatch

Since 2023, the new thing has been letting a chatbot choose the robot's face. That works when the chatbot picks from a short menu and something else does the animating. It goes wrong when the face arrives late, contradicts the words, flips back and forth, or keeps moving when nothing is happening.

The first public example came from Ameca's own account in early 2023: "Appropriate facial expressions in this video are selected by GPT3 - we also tried GPT4, the processing time with GPT4 was longer and made Ameca appear less responsive" ([Ameca on X](https://x.com/AmecaTheRobot/status/1641749840580280321)). Responsiveness beat smarter choices. The most instructive study is a peer-reviewed 2025 Ameca experiment with 28 participants and scripted dialogue. Expressions "were shown after the speech had ended" because rendering them during lip sync "often causes conflicts between the two processes". Participants noticed: "The expressions were not correctly timed; they didn't feel authentic." Wrong faces did harm too. One participant said a worried or startled face "made me think I'd asked a bad question". The robot could not pull its mouth corners down, and expressions looked "less pronounced" on the physical face than in the virtual preview ([Kirjavainen et al., Mindtrek '25](https://lacris.ulapland.fi/ws/files/46321884/3757980.3757996.pdf)). In a 2023 Furhat study with 47 participants, GPT-3.5 predicted the robot's emotion each turn, and congruent expressions were rated significantly more human-like and appropriate than incongruent ones ([Mishra et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10722897/)).

The format Frank already uses has direct precedent. Galatolo and Winkle (2025) had language models write inline `[GEST]` tags carrying a gesture label and an intensity between the words of speech. A 70-billion-parameter model produced invalid tags only about **3 %** of the time, while 8B and smaller models failed 27–62 % of the time with prompting alone. Furhat sometimes produced odd combinations such as smiling while talking ([Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2025.1581024/full)). Other systems sit at one end or the other of Frank's design. RIKEN's Nikola has GPT-4o return three labels from 63 authored expressions. It swaps whole faces at the label level, "cannot adjust individual action units", and ran smoothly in only 327 of 624 sessions ([Fujii et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2025.1728647/full)). Johns Hopkins' Xpress had GPT-4 write face-animation code, which took about 7 minutes per 500-word story and so had to be done in advance. In live conversation it fell back to a bank of 13 emotions at 2 intensities, showed each for the first 3 s of speech and then reset to neutral, and its authors report intensity mismatches and timing that felt "a bit too timely" ([arXiv 2503.00283](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.00283v1)). Google's GenEM has GPT-4 write code over a library of predefined robot skills. The multi-step version beat a single call, but no latency is reported and the robot had no face ([arXiv 2401.14673](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.14673v2)). A Ghent multi-party Furhat system averaged **1.35 s from the user's turn to the robot's reply, sometimes nearly 5 s**. It drove gaze from perception, not from the LLM, and gaze aimed at someone other than the addressee confused participants ([Abbo et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15496v2)). Language models are weak with emotion numbers: given valence and arousal values, they "struggle to classify VA values into discrete emotion categories" ([Mehra, Laban & Gunes 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3719160.3737618)). That is a good reason to keep Claude on labels.

Frank's tags have one structural advantage. They arrive in the text stream before the audio they belong to, and `frank/voice.py` already turns each face tag into an ElevenLabs v3 delivery tag (`[vulnerable]` becomes `[hesitant]`, `[concentrating]` becomes `[thoughtful]`), so his face and voice share a single cause. Convai's documentation names the alternatives. A lexicon classifier on the streamed text is "Responsive" and can change the face mid-reply; an LLM reading the finished reply is "Accurate" but arrives later ([Convai](https://docs.convai.com/api-docs/plugins-and-integrations/convai-unity-sdk/features/emotion/how-the-emotion-system-works)). Self-tagging beats both on latency. Cloud emotion measurement makes a poor dependency. Hume set June 14, 2026 as the last day of its expression-measurement API, which included the prosody model, and now offers a separate limited-release product ([Hume FAQ](https://dev.hume.ai/docs/expression-measurement/faq); [Hume overview](https://dev.hume.ai/docs/expression-measurement/overview)). Movement that is not about anything also costs a robot. In a multi-session study of an LLM-powered robot, observers logged expressions unrelated to the conversation and found "no habituation effect in reducing perceptions of creepiness or uncanny feelings" ([PMC12170534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12170534/)).

**How new is this?** The core techniques are 20–30 years old. Language models choosing expressions date only from 2023. The table sorts the history by what Frank should do with each piece.

The notes found no published system that drives a physical servo face from a continuous emotion state fed by LLM tags, layered under authored gaze and blink behaviour. Engineered Arts' and Hanson's technical documentation could not be read, so similar commercial work may exist unpublished. Each piece has strong precedent, which keeps the engineering risk low, and the novelty lies in the integration and in scheduling expressions against the voice. The open problems the sources name are latency, matching intensity to context, label-level granularity, lip sync on plosives and rounded vowels, silicone that will not wrinkle, uncanny reactions, and characters that die with their cloud. Embodied's Moxie could do little beyond blink after its servers went offline on 30 January 2025 ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2024/12/10/moxie-kids-robot-shuts-down)). Frank's idle, gaze, blink and thinking behaviour should therefore never depend on the network.

## A hybrid of PAD mood, tag mixtures and FACS poses fits Frank best

Psychology describes emotions in two main ways. One treats an emotion as a position on a map, with axes such as pleasant to unpleasant, calm to excited, and in control to not in control. The other treats emotions as named categories such as "surprise". Maps are good at smooth change and at moods that drift underneath. Categories are good at faces people can actually recognise. Frank needs both: a map for his slow mood and for how fast and how big things move, and named faces for what the audience reads. A third tool, FACS, names the individual muscle movements of the face, and it fits Frank's servos almost one to one. A fourth family, appraisal theory, works out which emotion a situation should cause, but Claude already does that job.

**PAD (pleasure, arousal, dominance) is the best-documented continuous space.** Published word coordinates on a −1..+1 scale include surprised (0.40, 0.67, −0.13), excited (0.62, 0.75, 0.38) and annoyed (−0.28, 0.17, 0.04). Those come from Russell and Mehrabian's 1977 norms as reproduced in a 2021 table, and the researchers flag two odd values in that extraction ([Kim et al., arXiv 2104.10117](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.10117)). Two dynamic emotion models run on PAD: ALMA ([Gebhard 2005](https://cs.huji.ac.il/course/2005/aisemin/articles2006/docs/pa1a3_29.pdf)) and WASABI ([Becker-Asano & Wachsmuth 2010](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cs886-affect/papers/WASABIAAMAS2010.pdf)). For Frank, though, **no published coordinates exist for curious, skeptical, confused or vulnerable**. WASABI puts "concentrated" at the origin as a "non-emotional state" shown with a neutral face. With proposed anchors, concentrating, neutral and, partly, curious and skeptical all sit within about 0.4 of the origin, so choosing the nearest face on the map would flicker between them. Ribeiro and Paiva add a second warning: a PAD transition from emotion A to emotion B "may go through an emotion C, which can be invalid" ([Ribeiro & Paiva, HRI 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)). The third axis earns its place, because dominance is what separates Frank's states. Skeptical and amused are high-control states; confused and vulnerable are low-control ones. Three independent lines of work keep such an axis: Kismet's stance, PAD's dominance and the Geneva Emotion Wheel's control axis. The Wheel also models intensity as distance from the hub and lets respondents pick blends of several emotion families ([Geneva Emotion Wheel](https://www.unige.ch/cisa/gew)), which is the right shape for Frank's synthesiser: one weight per named face, with several active at once. Cognitive states also deserve full faces. Rozin and Cohen found confusion and "think-concentrate" among the most frequently reported natural expressions. They found confusion "characterized principally by facial movements around the eyes" and the most common label for asymmetric faces ([Rozin & Cohen 2003](https://scilit.com/publications/c731c444dab2c5e917bfb788672f4962)). Tutoring studies tie confusion to brow lowering plus lid tightening, AU4 + AU7 ([Grafsgaard et al.](https://typeset.io/pdf/automatically-recognizing-facial-expression-predicting-1nb7yjn8a5.pdf)).

FACS supplies the render vocabulary. AU1 raises the inner brow, AU2 the outer brow, and AU4 lowers the brow. AU5 raises the upper lid and AU7 tightens the lids. AU12 pulls the lip corners up and AU15 pulls them down. AU25 and AU26 part the lips and drop the jaw. Each action unit has an intensity scale from A (trace) to E (maximum) and left, right and unilateral codes, and contempt, R12A + R14A, is the textbook one-sided expression ([FACS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_Action_Coding_System)). Frank's four brow servos can produce AU1, AU2 and AU4 independently on each side, which covers most of what distinguishes his cognitive tags. The corner servos stand in for AU12 against AU15, the lids for AU5 against AU7, and the jaw for AU25/26. AU6, the cheek raise in a real smile, has no actuator, so a slight lift of the lower lids has to imply it. The popular "skeptical single eyebrow raise" has no primary FACS source the researchers could find. It is folk knowledge, and Frank's bible asks for it anyway.

Appraisal theories such as OCC and EMA mostly answer "which emotion, how strong", and Claude already answers the first half, so a full appraisal engine would duplicate work. Two small pieces are worth keeping. WASABI separates "cognition", which triggers an emotion, from "embodiment", which computes continuous dynamics, and that is exactly the Claude-to-engine split. EMA has a one-line mood-congruence rule: an emotion's intensity becomes its own intensity plus the current mood's leaning toward it ([Marsella & Gratch 2009](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/marsella2009ema.pdf)). Applied to Frank, his dry, disdainful baseline makes skeptical and irritated faces come easily and makes excitement take more to trigger.

**ALMA and WASABI publish dynamics you can implement directly.** ALMA uses three timescales: emotions that decay, moods that drift, and a personality that sets the mood's resting point. Its default mood is computed from Big Five traits. It names PAD's eight octants, and the octant with −P, −A, +D is "Disdainful". Its published constants are long: emotion decay of 20 s in the example config and "for example, 1 minute" in the text, a mood return of 10 to 20 minutes (the paper contradicts itself here), and an update every 500 ms ([Gebhard 2005](https://cs.huji.ac.il/course/2005/aisemin/articles2006/docs/pa1a3_29.pdf)). The researchers checked ALMA's personality formula against Mehrabian's regressions and found that its neuroticism terms carry the signs of emotional stability ([Mehrabian 1996](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/mehrabian1996analysis.pdf)). With stability in place of neuroticism, illustrative traits for Frank (open 0.6, conscientious 0.3, extraverted 0.0, agreeable −0.4, stable 0.3) give a resting mood of about **(−0.18, −0.20, +0.33)**, "slightly disdainful", which matches his brief. WASABI runs a spring-mass system on emotional valence, with a slower mood that follows it and a "boredom" term that rises when nothing happens. It projects these to PAD as P = (x + y)/2 and A = |x| ([WASABI manual](https://becker-asano.de/index.php/research/wasabi/78-wasabi-manual?start=3)). It weights each named emotion by distance from the current point, with an activation threshold (0.64 in the primary-only condition) and a saturation threshold (0.2). In its worked example, a cognitively triggered "very fearful" state fades back toward "fearful" within about 10 s with no further input ([WASABI 2010](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cs886-affect/papers/WASABIAAMAS2010.pdf)). The manual's spring has no damping term, so it would oscillate forever. Frank's version needs damping, critical damping for most emotions or slightly under-damped for a little rebound after surprise. Richer dynamics make a measurable difference: viewers judged WASABI's MAX agent **27.5 years old with secondary emotions against 19.8 without** (n = 23, p = 0.025) ([WASABI 2010](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cs886-affect/papers/WASABIAAMAS2010.pdf)). A mood running underneath the emotions changes how grown-up a character seems.

Two vendor designs show the same ideas in production. Convai runs a persistent **mood** channel that reactions "never overwrite" and a **transient** channel that "rises and decays with each signal, then settles toward the mood", with an optional "short overshoot on arrival". Its emotion output goes to a shared facial compositor that "resolves overlapping claims on the mouth during speech", and "an unresolved label logs one warning and falls back to neutral" ([Convai](https://docs.convai.com/api-docs/plugins-and-integrations/convai-unity-sdk/features/emotion/how-the-emotion-system-works)). NVIDIA's Audio2Face blends an externally supplied "preferred" emotion with the emotion it infers from audio. `preferred_emotion_strength` defaults to 0.5, and further controls cover overall strength, contrast and a cap on how many emotions engage at once ([NVIDIA A2F config](https://docs.nvidia.com/ace/audio2face-3d-microservice/1.3/text/deployment/container-config.html)). Its smoothing control runs from "no smoothing at all (can be jittery)" to "emotion values not updated over time" ([NVIDIA A2F tuning](https://docs.nvidia.com/ace/audio2face-3d-microservice/1.0/text/param-tuning.html)). Its Audio2Emotion model may not be used outside the Audio2Face project ([NGC](https://catalog.ngc.nvidia.com/orgs/nim/nvidia/containers/audio2face-3d/1)), so Frank should take the blending idea and measure the loudness and pitch of his own voice locally.

Blending named faces has three known traps, all visible in FACS. First, adding faces can create a third expression nobody chose. Curious (AU1 + 2, brows up) mixed with irritated (AU4, brows down and together) gives AU1 + 2 + 4 on the inner brow, which is the fear and worry brow. Second, opposite actions cancel on a shared servo. AU12 and AU15 are the same corner servo pushed in opposite directions, so amused plus vulnerable produces a blank mouth. Third, mixing a symmetric face with an asymmetric one at equal weight halves the asymmetry, and asymmetry is what reads as skeptical and confused. The researchers found no study measuring how people perceive linearly blended robot expressions, so these traps have to be tested on the head. The evidence that does exist favours recognisable named poses at full intensity, with continuous movement between them. On EMYS, after animation principles were applied, over 90 % of 61 viewers got at least two of three intensities right for anger, fear and sadness, over 60 % for surprise and joy, and under 50 % for disgust ([Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)).

The verdict for Frank is a hybrid. PAD carries the slow mood, the personality resting point and the arousal that sets speed and size, using ALMA's octants and personality mapping and WASABI's damped dynamics and distance weights. The face itself is a weight per named tag, as on the Geneva wheel, rendered through FACS-style poses blended one region at a time as on Kismet. An asymmetry channel survives the blend, and a Convai-style split between transient and mood decides what the face settles back to. Pure Russell circumplex, Ekman's categories alone, OCC, CPM and EMA's planner all add little beyond that.

## Human measurements supply the animator's constants: 300 ms blinks, 3.5 s thinking glances

The small behaviours that make a face feel alive have been measured: how often people blink in conversation, how long a blink takes, where people look while thinking compared with listening, and when eyebrows rise during speech. These numbers can go into the code as starting values and then be tuned by eye on the real head. Animation craft adds the rules for using them: ease every move in and out, never snap, keep something gently alive at all times, give each feature its own timing, and break left-right symmetry.

The animation principles that matter for faces come with evidence. Ribeiro and Paiva map Disney's principles onto robots. Slow-in/slow-out with soft blending between animations ("Merging Logic" on the iCat) is "one of the major ones to apply". Squash and stretch mostly does not apply to rigid parts. Secondary action means random blinks and slow breathing-like motion. On solid drawing they write: "The main concept to get from this principle is asymmetry. Faces rarely exhibit the same expression on the left and right sides." They also report that a robot's acceleration correlates with how aroused it seems ([Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)). Takayama, Dooley (Pixar) and Ju found that visible forethought before an action raised appeal (4.83 against 4.27 on a 7-point scale) and approachability (5.05 against 4.54), and that a reaction to the outcome raised perceived competence (4.72 against 3.86), even though it did not make the action easier to read. Their robot was a faceless PR2, so the numbers transfer to Frank only in direction ([Takayama et al. 2011](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf)). Exaggeration raised engagement and recall, but only once it was strong enough to notice ([Gielniak & Thomaz 2012](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)). Mori warned that movement "changes the shape of the uncanny valley graph by amplifying the peaks and valleys" ([IEEE Spectrum translation](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-uncanny-valley)). For a silicone monster head, that makes jerky servo steps, frozen stillness and features moving in lockstep the real dangers.

Several timing values Frank needs have no published source. The brow flash's rise, hold and fall, the millisecond gap between lids and brows in surprise, conversational fixation lengths and the latency of hobby servos all fall in that gap, so the figures given for them later are design guesses to tune.

For smoothing, the standard modern answer is Daniel Holden's **critically damped spring**. It is frame-rate independent, it is tuned with a single "half-life" per channel, it never overshoots, and its companion technique, **inertialization**, blends from one clip to the next by decaying the offset between them instead of snapping ([Holden, "Spring-It-On"](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)). Deliberate point-to-point gestures can use a minimum-jerk curve, the fifth-degree polynomial with zero velocity and acceleration at both ends ([Duarte notebook](https://nbviewer.jupyter.org/github/demotu/BMC/blob/master/notebooks/MinimumJerkHypothesis.ipynb)). In normalised form those end conditions give s = 10t³ − 15t⁴ + 6t⁵. Minimum jerk is also Reachy Mini's default interpolation ([Reachy Mini API](https://huggingface.co/docs/reachy_mini/main/API/reachymini)). Per-servo speed and acceleration caps are standard in Pololu's Maestro, whose acceleration "ramps velocity rather than capping it" ([Pololu](https://www.pololu.com/docs/0J40/4.b)), and in Bottango's per-servo `maxPWMChangePerSec` ([Bottango protocol](https://docs.bottango.com/apis-and-comms/bottango-protocol/commands/)). The hardware traps are well known. ServoEasing reports a deadband of about 0.5° on digital servos that makes slow moves stutter, and warns that a controller cannot know a servo's starting position, so the first move after power-up can be abrupt ([ServoEasing](https://github.com/ArminJo/ServoEasing)). A servo holding against a load hums. On the Pico, `duty_u16(0)` releases one channel while `deinit()` kills both channels of a PWM slice ([MicroPython rp2](https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/rp2/quickref.html)). The MG90S datasheet gives a 700 mA stall current ([TinyTronics](https://www.tinytronics.nl/product_files/000263_Data_Sheet_of_MG90S_Analog_Servo_Motor.pdf)), while a vendor estimates 1.2–1.6 A at 6 V ([Kpower](https://www.kpower.com/insight_bldc/7870.html)). About twenty servos stalling together would therefore draw roughly 14–32 A, which is worth checking against the 20 A buck in the power design. Timing on the laptop is good enough: Python 3.11 and later sleep on a high-resolution Windows timer ([python-checkins](https://www.python.org/pipermail/python-checkins/2021-November/173356.html)), and `python-sounddevice` reports the time each audio buffer reaches the DAC, so the face can be locked to the sound card's clock ([python-sounddevice](https://python-sounddevice.readthedocs.io/api/streams.html)).

## Frank's engine: an orchestrator for when, a synthesiser for what, an animator for how

The engine has three parts, each with one job. The **orchestrator** is the stage manager. It knows what is happening in the conversation (someone arrived, they are talking, Frank is thinking, Frank is speaking, someone cut in) and exactly when each of Frank's expression tags should land against his voice. The **synthesiser** is the actor's inner state. It keeps a slow mood and a mixture of current feelings, each rising and fading on its own clock, and turns that mixture into a target face. The **animator** is the puppeteer's hands. Fifty times a second it combines that target face with blinks, glances, lip sync and quick reflexes, and moves every servo smoothly inside its limits. Claude never touches a servo, and the engine never decides what Frank feels.

In code, this fits in a small `frank/face/` package next to `chat.py`, with `orchestrator.py`, `synth.py`, `animator.py`, a `channels.py` map and a folder of JSON clips, running as threads inside the chat program. Information flows one way. Events come in: text deltas and tags from the reply stream, piece starts and 20 ms audio slices from the voice player, people, speakers, arrivals, departures, moves and darkness from `frank/eyes.py`, and barge-ins from the listener. The orchestrator turns them into timed **intentions**: a tag with a start time and intensity, a state profile, a gaze target. The synthesiser updates its state every tick and outputs a target per face channel plus an arousal value. The animator composes its layers and writes to one serial port.

### The orchestrator fires every tag against the audio clock

The orchestrator owns the nine controller states from the character bible, built as a hierarchical `transitions` machine with timeouts. Each state is a profile that sets the gaze behaviour, the blink rate, the idle amplitude, how much expression is allowed, and a short entry reaction. Takayama's result that a visible reaction to an outcome raised perceived competence is the reason every transition gets one. The starting profiles below combine the measured values from the previous sections with Frank's bible. The entry reactions are character choices to tune, not published values.

| State | Entered when | Gaze | Blinks | Face and entry reaction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Idle | Nobody engaged | Look-around fixations; boredom slowly lengthens them | About 17/min | Mood only; boredom lowers arousal, with a floor so he never looks asleep |
| Person detected | `eyes.py` reports an arrival | Glance once the Disney-style curiosity score crosses a threshold | Gaze-evoked | Faint curious tint |
| Greeting | Frank engages the visitor | Engage: triangle saccades every 0.1–0.5 s | Blink on the big shift | `brow_anim.py`'s "greeting": brows rise, eyes widen, he studies the visitor for about 0.7 s |
| Listening | The visitor is speaking | Mostly mutual gaze; a sideways aversion about every 7.2 s for about 1.1 s | 0.25–0.5 s after the visitor's pauses; a ~600 ms long blink as "got it" | Mood plus backchannels (a soft brow flash or knit, or a long blink with a slight smile) on low-pitch cues, no more than one per 3–5 s, varied |
| Thinking | The visitor's turn ends and Claude is working | Upward cognitive aversion of about 3.5 s, returning as audio starts | Drops toward 4–10/min, with a burst on return | Light concentrating face timed with the "Hmm." filler; in the last moments before audio, a 30 % preview of the reply's first tag |
| Speaking | The first slice of reply audio plays | Mutual gaze with small sideways aversions about every 4.75 s at 0.4 amplitude; mutual gaze held through the last 2.4 s of the final sentence | About 26/min, placed at phrase boundaries | Tag transients, at most one brow beat per phrase |
| Interrupted | `voice.stop()` on barge-in | Snap to the speaker | Reflex blink | Brow flinch overlay, then Listening |
| Farewell | Departure | Follow the leaver, then hold briefly on the exit | One slow blink | Small brow lift, settle |
| Returning to idle | No one for a set time | Drift back to look-around | Normal | Transients decay to mood |

Thinking matters most, because the character bible says to "avoid arbitrary facial motion during response delays", and Ameca's GPT-4 trial showed that waiting time is what makes a robot seem unresponsive. With gaps of 1–5 s before a reply in comparable systems, an authored, upward, considering look is the behaviour viewers read as thoughtful. Andrist's robots that averted gaze were rated more thoughtful, and Engineered Arts reportedly authors a look-up-and-left for exactly this window, though that comes from a weak secondary source ([ai.miraheze.org](https://ai.miraheze.org/wiki/Ameca)).

**Tags fire on the audio clock, not when they are parsed.** A tag from Claude's stream is queued with the piece it belongs to and fires when that piece's first sample reaches the speaker, read from the sound card's DAC time, minus a small lead. Brows and lids should start about 150–250 ms before the tagged words, and the mouth corners should join later because speech owns the mouth. That lead is a design choice drawn from Emo's anticipation result and from how much visual lead viewers tolerate in speech. Today's code has a gap here. `voice.py` groups sentences into pieces of at least 30 characters for the first and 160 for the rest. It writes a v3 delivery tag into the text for every change of face, but it keeps only the first sentence's face for the mouth (`buf_face`). So a mid-piece `[amused]` changes Frank's voice and not his face. The fix is to carry a list of (character offset, tag) pairs for each piece and fire each one at its own time. The time is exact if the voice switches to the stream-with-timestamps endpoint. Otherwise it can be estimated from the fraction of audio played, as `voice.py` already does at 14 characters per second to guess what was heard before an interruption. Nobody has confirmed whether v3's alignment output includes or strips the bracketed delivery tags, so test that first. A **tag-only reply** becomes a silent beat: the expression plays at full intensity with gaze held on the speaker, holds for 1.5–3 s (inside the 0.5–4 s macro-expression range), then decays to mood. `[chuckle]` triggers a short clip of amused corners, a lid squint and a small brow lift, while the chuckle audio itself drives the jaw through the speech layer.

The orchestrator also enforces the rules that stop the face from looking frantic or fake. A new tag must hold for at least 1.0–1.5 s before another one can replace it, following Vector's rule against cycling rapidly between emotions; only surprised may cut in sooner. A repeated tag extends the hold and nudges the intensity up by 10–20 %, up to a cap, instead of replaying the clip, because the bible forbids "identical listening loops". A switch to an opposite-valence tag crossfades more slowly. Claude sends no intensity, so the orchestrator sets one: a base value per tag, a little more for a reply's opening tag or an exclamation mark, plus EMA-style mood congruence. Then it blends in the loudness and pitch movement of the actual v3 audio, the way Audio2Face blends a preferred emotion with an inferred one (start around 70 % tag and 30 % audio). Because Frank's voice runs at stability 0 ("Creative") and every take differs, this keeps the face from over-performing a flat delivery. A **"one thing at a time" budget** carries the bible's line that "Frank's face should not perform every movement at once". Feature onsets are staggered: eyes, then lids at +0–50 ms, brows at +50–150 ms, and mouth at +100–300 ms, with jitter, and one side leads the other by 30–80 ms. The staggering follows Namba's surprise data and Disney's eyes-first rule. The offsets themselves are design values. Claude should not be asked for numbers. Mehra's result and Galatolo's error rates both argue for labels. If intensity control is ever needed, a coarse three-level suffix such as `[amused:2]` matches what Galatolo and Convai use.

Gaze comes from perception, never from Claude. `eyes.py` already reports each face's yaw and pitch from Frank's eyes, so the orchestrator picks a target and the animator converts it to eye angles. The current speaker wins, because the Ghent study found that mismatched gaze confused people. Otherwise Disney's curiosity score decides, with habituation, so Frank's attention wanders believably between several visitors. Emotion biases gaze through the dominance axis. Skeptical and irritated hold a steady, direct stare with fewer saccades. Vulnerable softens into more frequent and lower aversions. Confused adds brief alternating glances. Concentrating looks away and returns "when ready", exactly as the bible's expression states describe. Other events get character reactions. Being moved earns surprise and then irritation. Darkness brings a slower look-around with slightly lowered lids. A departure brings the farewell profile. If the network drops, the orchestrator stays in Idle and Listening with the full alive layer running, which follows the Moxie lesson and the bible's "recover gracefully from network interruptions".

### The synthesiser keeps a mood and a mixture, never a single face

The synthesiser treats Frank's emotion as a continuous spectrum in two layers. The first is a slow **mood**, a point in PAD space with a resting home at about (−0.18, −0.20, +0.33), dry and slightly disdainful. The second is a set of ten **transient weights**, one per tag, each between 0 and 1. A tag never "switches the face". It raises one weight along an envelope with an attack time per tag, an optional small overshoot of 10–20 % (Convai's "micro-burst") for surprised, excited and amused, a hold, and a decay whose display half-life starts at 2–10 s, near WASABI's 10 s fade. ALMA's minute-long decays are too slow for visitors walking past. Each tag event also pulls the mood a small step toward that tag's anchor, in the spirit of ALMA's pull, and the mood relaxes home through a critically damped spring with a half-life of 1–5 minutes. Transients decay toward a **floor** rather than zero. The floor is a small "leakage" weight computed WASABI-style from the distance between the current mood and each tag's anchor, using WASABI's activation and saturation thresholds as starting points. When nothing is happening, Frank's face therefore settles into a faint version of whatever his mood is near, usually skeptical, instead of a blank neutral. A boredom term rises in silent Idle and lowers arousal, giving slower look-arounds and heavier lids, with a floor so he never looks asleep. Arousal, from the mood's A axis, the active tags and the measured audio, sets speed and size: higher arousal means shorter spring half-lives, larger idle motion and quicker saccades, since acceleration reads as arousal. All of this is cheap arithmetic at 25–50 Hz.

Rendering runs in pose space, never back through PAD. Each tag stores a full-intensity pose for each face region, in the EMYS style of one stored vector per emotion scaled down for lower intensities. The regions are the four brow ends, the upper and lower lids, a gaze bias and saccade style, the four corners, the lips, and a jaw floor. Within each region the active tags are blended by their weights, and the neutral weight takes up the remainder so that low intensity means close to rest. Three rules handle the blending traps. Above a weight of about 0.3, a tag that raises the inner brow and a tag that lowers it never share the brow region: the stronger one takes it outright, which prevents the accidental worry brow. Opposing pulls on one servo resolve by largest magnitude rather than a signed sum, which prevents the blank mouth. Asymmetry is stored as its own signed per-side term and added after the blend so it survives mixing. Frank also gets a favoured side, say his left brow as the skeptic's brow, with the occasional switch, so his one-sided looks feel like habits and not noise. During speech the speech layer owns the jaw and lips. The synthesiser's mouth output shrinks to a corner offset, which is what `mouth.py`'s `MOOD` table does today, so the brows, lids and gaze carry the emotion. The mouth expresses fully in pauses and in silent-face replies.

The ten tags need anchors and recipes. Four anchors are published values; the other six are proposals placed between published neighbours. The face recipes come from the character bible's expression states, the FACS evidence above and the poses already tuned in `brow_anim.py`.

### The animator composes layers and owns every servo

The animator runs a fixed 50 Hz loop on `time.perf_counter()` deadlines, never `sleep(0.02)`, which drifts. It feeds the measured time step into frame-rate-independent springs so that loop jitter never changes motion speed. Its layer stack adapts Disney's. L0 is the calibrated rest pose. L1 "alive" carries the blink scheduler (state-dependent rates, skewed random intervals with a refractory of about 1 s, and event blinks for big gaze shifts, Frank's own phrase ends, the visitor's pauses and reflexes), saccades of at least 1°, and slow simplex-noise drift of about 1–3 % of range on brows, corners and lids. It stays deliberately small, because idle motion that reads as unrelated to the conversation is a documented failure. L2 is the synthesiser's face. L3 holds the state behaviours: gaze modes, aversion schedules and the thinking look. L4 is speech: `mouth.py`'s jaw, lips and corners plus brow beats. L5 holds one-shot overlays such as the interruption flinch, the greeting flash and tag clips.

Composition follows fixed rules for each channel. Gaze is an override from the highest layer that claims it, plus L1's saccade offsets. Upper-lid openness is the smaller of the emotion's openness and the gaze-follow limit, multiplied by (1 − blink closure), so a blink always wins. Brows are the emotion pose plus additive speech beats and noise, clamped to the hard stops. The jaw takes the larger of speech and the emotion's jaw floor. Corners and lips are the emotion base plus speech shaping, with the speech weight crossfaded over 100–200 ms. Clips use Furhat's shape, named timed frames of named channels with an intensity, along with Disney's show-object algebra (group, sequence, loop, random pick, add, scale), so a brow flash can be scaled for exaggeration, added to an emotion, and varied so no two plays match. Clips tied to speech run on audio time, as every show-control tool does. Every clip starts from the current pose by inertialization, never from neutral.

Every channel passes through the same chain: target, then the soft-limit clamp, then the critical spring with that channel's half-life, then a speed and acceleration cap, then quantisation to 0.5–1°, then send-on-change outside a deadband of about 0.5°. Saccades and blink closures skip the spring and step straight to the target so the servo slews at full speed, because a human-speed saccade lasts only one or two 50 Hz frames. Brows and corners get the longer half-lives, which produces Disney's per-feature bandwidth and the bible's "distinct timing" for free. Channels carry abstract names (`brow_inner_L`, `lid_top_R`, `gaze_x`, `jaw`, `corner_upper_L`), calibrated from the poses saved in the slider windows (`eye_poses.json`, `mouth_poses.json`, the brow level, RAISE and LOWER). They are named by the character's side per the project rule, which differs from `brow_anim.py`'s bench comment "sides as seen from the front", so fix that in one place. A single writer owns the mouth Pico's serial port and reaches the eye-and-brow Pico through `e <pin> <angle>`. Eyes and lids go out at 50 Hz, while brows, lips and corners go out at 25 Hz, as `mouth.py` already does. At 50 Hz, around twenty channels of short ASCII lines come to under 10 kB/s over USB. Check the Pico-to-Pico UART's baud rate, since forwarded `e` lines share it.

Three physical details need measuring. First, latency. `mouth.py` delays each jaw target by 50 ms (`DELAY`) to line it up with the sound leaving the speaker, so the servo's own mechanical lag lands on top and the jaw trails the sound, which is the direction viewers notice. Filming a jaw step against a click with a phone in slow motion gives the servo lag. The engine should then analyse audio from the voice player's chunk queue ahead of playback, stamp each slice with its DAC time, and command the jaw that much early plus about 30–50 ms. Second, power. The animator should stagger large simultaneous moves across servos. Third, holding. Release with `duty_u16(0)` only the servos that hold position unpowered, keep lids and jaw under skin tension powered, and always re-attach from the last commanded angle. For resilience, `eye_anim.py` and `brow_anim.py` already run in MicroPython, so the Pico can fall back to local idle blinks and glances whenever laptop frames stop for more than about a second. Frank stays alive through a laptop stall.

### Building it in five steps from today's code

The first step is the animator core: the channel map, the single serial writer, the 50 Hz loop and the spring chain. `eye_anim.py` and `brow_anim.py` get ported in as the L1 and L2 producers, and `mouth.py` is refactored so its audio analysis feeds the speech layer instead of owning a port and a thread. The step ends when today's behaviour is reproduced with a CSV log of every channel per tick for plotting. The second step is the synthesiser, with the tag table above, envelopes, the mood floor and region blending, driven from `speech.parse` on a bench script with no LLM. The third step is the orchestrator: the states, audio-clock tag scheduling including mid-piece tags, gaze from `eyes.py`, and the thinking, listening and speaking profiles. The fourth step refines speech. It switches to timestamped streaming for brow beats on focus words and phrase-end blinks, adds closed-lip shapes for p, b and m and rounding for oo and w, the two classes Emo still struggles with, and adds audio-arousal modulation. The fifth step is tuning on the real head, with MediaPipe puppeteering to record clips and expression gains raised to beat the physical damping that left Ameca's expressions less pronounced than its on-screen preview. Each step should be checked against the failures this research documents: an expression arriving after the words, a face contradicting them, gaze on the wrong person, jitter at one smoothing extreme and a frozen face at the other, idle motion that seems unrelated, flip-flopping, everything moving at once, identical loops, the accidental worry brow, the blank mouth from cancelling corners, halved asymmetry, servo hum, and a brown-out on a big simultaneous move.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. Frank's expression shapes are mostly solved already: the character bible defines them, `brow_anim.py` has tuned the brow half, and the literature confirms the FACS logic behind them. The work that decides whether Frank seems alive is choreography in time: tags locked to the sound card's clock, an authored thinking look that fills the one to five seconds a cloud reply takes in comparable systems, gaze that follows whoever is speaking and looks away on human schedules, and blinks that fall at phrase ends and after the visitor's pauses. The Ameca study shows that a well-funded face with good shapes and bad timing still felt inauthentic to testers, and Andrist's held-floor result shows that timing alone changes how people behave toward the robot.

Frank's tag set also shapes the engine more than it first appears. Four of his ten tags (curious, concentrating, confused, vulnerable) have no Ekman prototype at all, skeptical has only a contempt-like stretch, and several of these cognitive states crowd the middle of any emotion map. Speech, meanwhile, claims his mouth most of the time he is emotional. Together these mean his emotional range will live in the brows, lids and gaze, rendered as named faces mixed by weight over a drifting mood, with the map used only for mood and speed. The brows are also what the bible names as his signature expression. The parts of this design that no source has tested are perceptual: how blended faces read on a silicone skin, how much gain the skin eats, and what servo lag does to lip sync. None of these can be settled from a desk, so the logging and puppeteering tools in steps one and five matter as much as the engine itself.
