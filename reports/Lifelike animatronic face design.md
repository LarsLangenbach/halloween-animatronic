# Wake Frank's twenty servos before adding more

Frank already has the face motions that research androids, Disney figures and the best hobby heads all buy first. Will Cogley's Robot Head α2.0.33 gives him **15 independent motions from 20 servos**: shared eye pan and tilt, four separate eyelids, an inner and an outer end on each brow, a jaw, four lip servos and four mouth-corner servos. That is the "core budget" of roughly 15–20 motions that nearly every expressive face spends before anything else, and it is in the same range as the roughly ten face functions journalists report for Disney's Hondo Ohnaka figure. Motors are not what Frank mostly lacks. A face performance engine built for NightHack on 2026-10-30 (`frank/engine/`) now drives his eyes, lids, brows and the emotion part of his mouth corners from his tags and his camera. That matters, because characters whose upper face stays still while they talk were rated more uncanny in perception studies and were even read as hostile. But the engine has so far been tuned on a drawing of the face and on bench sliders, not on the skinned head: it runs at **80 % of its poses' strength by design**, and none of the ranges it relies on have been measured. His cast Dragon Skin FX-Pro skin has **no anchors yet** (milestone M4 was never started), and the best-known professional flops, Disney's 2017 Trump figure and Sophia's "stiff and mechanical" performances, failed at the skin, the eyes and the timing rather than at motor count. A rough calculation from Smooth-On's own figures says a 5 mm FX-Pro band needs about **7.5 N to stretch 20 %**, which is more than an MG90S can pull comfortably, so the skin may swallow much of what the servos do. The plan that follows has four steps in order. First, check and tune the new engine on the skinned head, with heavier Karloff-style lids at rest, more lower-lid squint, deliberate asymmetry, more gain rather than less, and limits taken from measured stops; this needs no parts. Second, anchor the skin at the moving points, lock it along the fold lines, and measure it. Third, spend the four free Pico sockets on a sneer and a cheek lift, but only if the software stand-ins fail on the skinned face. Fourth, keep a neck and a second, zoned skin cast for the next version; a nod would need a spring, because the head's 1.5 N·m forward lean is more than an MG996R's stall torque. The ranking of motions and several priorities below are judgement calls built on indirect evidence. No published study switches off one face motion at a time on a physical robot and measures what is lost.

This report covers the physical face: which motions matter, the skin, the mechanisms and actuators, professional character craft, the Frankenstein character, and an audit of Frank as built. The software side (emotion models, blending, blink and gaze timing, lip-sync, LLM-driven expression and servo smoothing) is in the [earlier face-engine report](Robot%20face%20emotion%20animation.md). It is cited here where it answers part of the question and is not repeated. That design became Frank's face engine, specified in [FACE_ENGINE.md](../docs/FACE_ENGINE.md), so the software recommendations below are framed as next steps and tuning for that engine.

## Four traditions converged on the same fifteen-motion core

In plain terms: four groups have built lifelike faces. University labs build realistic androids, theme-park companies build performing figures, film creature shops build puppeted heads, and hobby makers build with cheap servos and silicone. Their budgets and motors differ a lot, but when they decide where to put motors they nearly all start in the same places: the eyes, the eyelids, eyebrows split into inner and outer parts, the jaw, and the mouth corners. Frank has all of these.

**Research androids** run from about 7 to 39 head degrees of freedom (DOF), and most expressive research heads sit at 20 to 35. A 2023 RIKEN comparison table lists Geminoid F at 12, SAYA at 24, Albert HUBO at 31, the Hashimoto life-mask face at 39 and Nikola at 35 ([Yang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05620)). The Japanese androids are mostly pneumatic, chosen because air moves quietly and smoothly. Nikola uses **29 air actuators for facial muscle actions** plus 3 for the head and 3 for the eyes, placed according to FACS, the system that codes the face by individual muscle movements. A certified FACS coder confirmed 17 action units (AUs) on it, but the nose wrinkle (AU9), chin raise (AU17) and lip tightener (AU23) failed "owing to the technical limitations of the silicone skin" ([Sato et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.800657/full)). Hanson's Sophia has 33 DOF in the head and face, driven by cords anchored under its foamed "Frubber" skin, and a 2024 paper says its motors cover only about half of the ARKit blendshapes, so big smiles and fine expressions are hard and viewers found its performances "stiff and mechanical" ([Sophia-in-Audition](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06978)). Engineered Arts' Ameca shows how unreliable published counts can be. A "27 DOF in the face" figure circulates without an official source ([AI Wiki](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/engineered_arts_ameca)), a benchmark paper gives 32 including the neck ([X2C](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.11146)), a search summary of the company's own docs gives 61 actuated DOF in total ([Engineered Arts docs](https://docs.engineeredarts.co.uk/guide/reference/hardware/mesmer_full_size_gen2)), and a peer-reviewed 2026 study says the Ameca it used had **13 controllable facial actuators** ([Schwarz et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2026.1798227/full)). Columbia's Eva is the closest relative of Frank: **12 MG90S face servos** in two printed servo banks, steel wires in Teflon Bowden tubes, a printed skull and a silicone mask, giving up to 15 mm of skin movement for about US$900 in total ([Faraj et al., HardwareX](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9041256)). Its successor Emo has 26 actuators under magnetically attached skin and learned to smile about 840 ms before the person it watches does ([Columbia Engineering](https://engineering.columbia.edu/news/robot-can-you-say-cheese)).

**Theme-park figures** spend face motors according to how close the audience stands. Journalists report that Disney's Na'vi Shaman (2017) has about 40 functions in the face alone ([BlogMickey relaying TechCrunch](https://blogmickey.com/2019/02/photos-video-hondo-audio-animatronic-figure-behind-the-scenes-testing-list-of-aas-coming-to-star-wars-galaxys-edge/)), with other sources giving 42 or 45 for the head ([RobotsGuide](https://robotsguide.com/robots/navishaman)). Hondo Ohnaka (2019), the showcase of Disney's all-electric A-1000 platform, reportedly has 51 functions, **only 10 of them in the face** ([Laughing Place](https://www.laughingplace.com/w/news/2019/02/28/hondo-animatronic-revealed-for-star-wars-galaxys-edge-at-disney-parks/)). These are journalists' counts, so treat them as orders of magnitude. Garner Holt Productions' 2017 Lincoln bust has **45 facial functions, 12 of them in the lips** and 4 per eye, with the skin held by "dozens of unique flexible magnetic grippers" so it can be swapped in under two minutes ([MiceChat](https://micechat.com/168204-blurring-mechanical-line-ghps-new-animatronic-head-offers-glimpse-future-expressive-characters/); [InPark Magazine](https://www.inparkmagazine.com/its-a-dimensional-thing-garner-holt/amp/)). The other end of the range is Disney's free-roaming Olaf (2025). A trade report says a single actuator drives his whole mouth and his eyes have yaw plus a linked pitch-and-blink, and the effort went into motion quality and pre-recorded gags ([Attractions Magazine](https://attractionsmagazine.com/how-imagineers-brought-a-free-roaming-robotic-olaf-to-life/)).

**Film creature shops** solved the face with people rather than programs. Henson's Performance Control System let one puppeteer run up to 32 servos, and its Performance Editor (1990) recorded, edited and replayed a live take; those figures come from a fan wiki and should be checked ([Muppet Wiki](https://muppet.fandom.com/wiki/Henson_Performance_Control_System)). On the *Five Nights at Freddy's* film, off-screen Henson puppeteers ran eyebrows, arms and necks by remote control ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/five-nights-at-freddys-interview-puppets-jim-henson-1850943504)). The Stan Winston School forum is the best public record of creature-shop mechanics. One wolf-head build uses a single servo and a roller arm under the nose that gives a breathing twitch, a sniff or a full snarl depending on travel ([SWS wolf head](https://forums.stanwinstonschool.com/discussion/comment/7448)).

**Hobby and recent open builds** have converged on 9 g servos in large numbers. Cogley's own 2024 silicone head used 14 servos and held its skin with magnets in small clamp pieces ([Arduino Blog](https://blog.arduino.cc/2024/12/09/this-frighteningly-realistic-animatronic-head-features-expressive-silicone-skin/)). InMoov's i2 head uses 13 face servos, Velcro over most of the face, glue at the eyelids and some magnets ([Make:](https://makezine.com/article/technology/robotics/skin-deep-evolving-inmoovs-facial-expressions-with-ai)). Two 2026 conference heads reach **21 face DOF on 9 g servos** ([BAAI/Tsinghua, RSS 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11688); [NUS/Moratuwa, AIM 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.17042)). The second of those holds its skin with snaps at the points that move and magnets where the skin stays still, and its weakest expression was fear at 70 % recognition.

The common core across WE-4RII, Nikola, Sophia and the hobby heads is eye pan and tilt, upper lids, split brows, a jaw and mouth corners that go up and down. The 25–35 DOF heads then add cheeks (AU6, AU13), a lip stretcher (AU20), a pucker (AU18/22), an upper-lip raiser (AU10) and the nose (AU9). Nikola shows that these second-tier motions are the ones silicone skin defeats first ([Sato et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.800657/full)). Frank's 15 motions put him at the full core and a little beyond it, since his four lids and two servos per mouth corner are more than most hobby faces have. Hardware count is not his bottleneck.

## Eyes and brows carry the feeling, and a still upper face reads as menace

In plain terms: people read emotion mostly from two places, the eyes with the brows and the mouth. Sad and scared faces are read from the eyes, happy and disgusted faces from the mouth, and surprise from both. When a realistic character talks with a frozen forehead, brows and lids, people find it creepy and even suspect it of being aggressive. So the upper face is worth far more than it costs, and for Frank it is already built.

A 2017 study revealed faces tile by tile and found that observers "were mostly relying on the eye and mouth regions", with expressions lying on a continuum from eye-led sadness and fear to mouth-led disgust and happiness ([Wegrzyn et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5426715)). The most directly useful finding for a monster head comes from Tinwell and colleagues: characters lacking upper-face movement during speech were rated more uncanny, the effect was strongest for fear and surprise, and it was **strongest of all for realistic, human-like male characters with still eyelids, brows and forehead** ([Tinwell et al.](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/63140615/Facial_expression_of_emotion_and_perception.pdf)). Even a small lapse such as missing lid or brow movement made participants suspect a character of "aggressive tendencies" ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/78783)), and Tinwell's own advice for making a zombie creepier is to "reduce movement in the upper face, including the eyes, the eyebrows and the forehead" ([The Naked Scientists](https://www.thenakedscientists.com/node/199164)). For Frank this works as a dial. A frozen upper face makes a corpse and a moving one makes a sympathetic creature, and a scare level of 2/10 ([CHARACTER.md](../docs/CHARACTER.md#L52)) puts him firmly on the moving side.

The cheek raise of a real smile (AU6, the Duchenne marker) is the one secondary motion with direct evidence that it changes how genuine an expression feels. When the muscle around the eye was temporarily paralysed with botulinum toxin, smiles made while it still worked were rated more felt, spontaneous and happy ([Etcoff et al. 2021](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10120320/1/Etcoff%20et%20al%282021%29.pdf)), and Krumhuber's group found the marker makes "a unique direct contribution" to perceived genuineness beyond intensity ([Krumhuber et al.](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10111350/9/Krumhuber_20200306_AU6ms_VersionAccepted.pdf)). The counter-argument is that AU6 mostly signals intensity, since strong negative faces contain it too ([Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01126/full); [Affective Science](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42761-020-00030-w)). Either way, raising the lower lids narrows the eye in the same direction, and Frank's four independent lids can do that today.

Low-DOF faces lose anger and disgust first. In the 2026 Ameca study (13 face actuators, 40 viewers), recognition on the physical robot was 65 % for surprise but **12.5 % for anger and 22.5 % for disgust**; the authors blame "a limited number of movable components", although the paper's abstract and its stated differences do not fully agree ([Schwarz et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2026.1798227/full)). Nikola recognised all six basic emotions above chance, but its happiness scored 90.0 % against 98.2 % for human faces, a gap the authors put down mainly to **"low facial expression intensity"** ([Sato et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.800657/full)). Getting full travel through the skin is worth as much as adding motions. Expert FACS poses are not the ceiling either: tuning Nikola's actuator mix against viewer ratings raised four of six emotions, and the authors note they left out asymmetric expressions "for simplicity" and suspect those would be more convincing ([Yang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05620)).

Head motion matters too. Head movement during emotional speech changes how listeners perceive the face's emotion, especially valence and activation ([Busso et al. 2007](https://msp.utdallas.edu/publications/Busso_2007.pdf)), and on a minimal robot head nodding, shaking and tilting all shifted perception, while moving the head before speaking raised naturalness ([IJSR 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12369-024-01196-0)). Where a study crossed a smile with a head tilt, though, the smile had the larger effect (RO-MAN 2024, search summary) ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/385416056_The_Feeling_of_Kawaii_toward_a_Robot's_Head-Tilting_Motion_Effects_of_Speed_Direction_and_Accompanying_Hand_Gestures)). Timing between face parts carries a warning. Asynchrony between face parts raised uncanniness for human and android faces in one study ([RIKEN, Diel et al. 2023](https://grp.riken.jp/en/news/20231220/)), while another found it lowered ratings of human faces but left android faces unaffected ([RIKEN 2023](https://grp.riken.jp/en/news/20231007/)). The earlier report recommends a small, ordered stagger (eyes, then lids, then brows, then mouth) based on how genuine surprise unfolds ([Namba et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7873236)). The two findings pull in different directions, so keep any stagger small, in that natural order, and check it on the head.

The ranking below combines these findings. It is a **design judgement**, not a measured result: recognition and uncanniness stand in for "fun", which no study measures.

| Rank (judgement) | Motion (FACS) | Why it ranks here | Frank today |
|---|---|---|---|

| 1 | Brows, inner and outer ends per side (AU1, AU2, AU4) | Sadness, fear, surprise and anger are eye-and-brow led; Frank's named signature | Fitted (4 servos), driven by the engine; travel unproven, one servo weak, TPU bars unconfirmed |

Four of the top six rows are already in Frank's head, and since 2026-10-30 the engine drives all of them except the lips. What they lack now is tuning on the real head, plus a skin that lets them show.

## Professionals treat the face as an actor, and famous flops failed in the skin and eyes

In plain terms: Disney and the creature shops think of a face as a performer. The animation is designed by eye by people trained as animators, or performed live by a puppeteer who usually also does the voice. When famous figures have failed, the trouble was almost never the body. It was skin that didn't follow the skull underneath, a mouth that didn't move the skin around it, and eyes that didn't look at anything.

Disney's A-1000 page describes its figures as "actors" giving "believable and emotive performances in the grand tradition of Disney animation", built with tools for "more direct translation from artist's hand to high-fidelity performance" ([Walt Disney Imagineering: A-1000](https://sites.disney.com/waltdisneyimagineering/a-1000/)). The job title for the people who program them is "Show Programmer, Figure Animator" ([Disney Careers](https://emplois.disneycareers.com/emploi/glendale/principal-show-programmer-figure-animator/391/87515800208)). The Lumiere pipeline had feature animators choreograph on a virtual rig, flagged the physical figure's limits in software, and let the artists rework those spots before transfer ([Pixels to Parks, SIGGRAPH 2013](https://disneyanimation.com/publications/pixels-to-parks-new-animation-techniques-for-fantasyland/)). When Imagineers say what makes a face come alive, they name small things. Joe Rohde reportedly singled out the Shaman's "little movements in the cheek, tiny movements in the eyelid" (a search-summary quote from a page that could not be opened) ([WDWMAGIC](https://wdwmagic.com/attractions/navi-river-journey/news/01may2017-video---disneys-most-sophisticated-animatronic-is-part-of-navi-river-journey-in-pandora---the-world-of-avatar.htm)). For the 2025 Walt Disney figure, the team traced Walt's famous eye glint to a corneal bulge and built it in, and Tom Fitzgerald said Walt's "very expressive" eyebrows were beyond what earlier figures could do ([WDWNT](https://wdwnt.com/2025/07/imagineering-shares-details-of-walt-disney-animatronic-show-moonshots)). On Olaf, the team kept the cartoon's non-physical motion instead of correcting it toward physics ([WDWNT](https://wdwnt.com/2025/12/disney-research-hub-shares-technical-details-behind-new-olaf-animatronic/)). Imagineer Darrin Hughes has said that costume movement "absorbs a little bit of the non-natural motion" of a figure (search summary) ([Jim Hill Media](https://jimhillmedia.com/marvelous-mechanized-magic-kingdom-event-dispelled-many-myths-about-audio-animatronics/?amp=1)). The Shaman also malfunctioned publicly in 2021, a reminder that every extra face function is something else to maintain ([WDWNT](https://wdwnt.com/2021/08/video-shaman-of-songs-audio-animatronic-malfunctions-on-navi-river-journey-at-disneys-animal-kingdom/)).

Garner Holt bets on skin. Holt has called the skin "the real star of the figure" ([MiceChat](https://micechat.com/168204-blurring-mechanical-line-ghps-new-animatronic-head-offers-glimpse-future-expressive-characters/)), his Lincoln's silicone is designed to form wrinkles and creases, and he describes its expressions as exaggerated on purpose, because the head is "designed to show off just how much expression can be exhibited" (search summary) ([InPark Magazine](https://www.inparkmagazine.com/its-a-dimensional-thing-garner-holt/amp/); [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/?p=1225899)). Henson's philosophy is the opposite end. A character feels alive because a live performer drives it and reacts to the room ([Nerdly](https://www.nerdly.co.uk/?p=12522)), and the face performer almost always also voices it (search summary) ([Gizmodo AU](https://gizmodo.com.au/?p=767743)). Frank's architecture (`frank/engine/`) already maps onto both. The language model speaks and chooses the feeling in real time, which is the Henson performer-and-voice role, while a local engine performs the motion, which is the Disney animator's role. The Performance Editor model of recording a live take, cleaning it and replaying it fits canned "signature bits" that the runtime can trigger.

Puppeteers' lip-sync rules are concrete: open the mouth once per syllable rather than flapping ([Puppet Nerd](https://puppetnerd.com/puppetry-101/)), open on the sound so words are not "eaten", and move the lower jaw more than the top (Steve Axtell, search summary) ([Axtell Expressions](https://axtell.com/?p=11238)). CG practice adds "animate the lip-sync first; then go in behind it and modify the face to fit the emotion", with "relatively few holds" ([AWN](https://www.awn.com/print/vfxworld/inspired-3d-lip-sync-and-facial-animation-part-1)). The earlier report turns these into timing numbers for the audio clock ([earlier report](Robot%20face%20emotion%20animation.md)).

The failures point the same way. An animatronics expert consulted by MiceChat said Disney's 2017 Trump figure's skin "doesn't seem to be adhering properly to the underskull", its mouth did not move the skin convincingly, and its eyes "diverged" and did not look focused, while "the body and arms move very well and are programmed exceptionally well" ([MiceChat](https://www.micechat.com/181457-awkward-animatronic-donald-trump-joins-hall-presidents/)); Disney fitted a new face in 2025 ([WDWNT](https://wdwnt.com/2025/06/disney-updates-donald-trump-animatronic-for-the-hall-of-presidents-following-hillary-clinton-controversy/)). Critics called Sophia "a chatbot with a face" ([Singularity Hub](https://singularityhub.com/2018/02/18/what-roboticists-are-learning-from-the-first-generation-of-lifelike-humanoid-robots/)), which is the trap for any talking head whose face gives little back while it talks fluently. The research version of the uncanny valley supports the craft view: realistic faces became most unpleasant only when they had abnormal features such as bizarre eyes, so realism was necessary but not sufficient (search summary) ([Seyama & Nagayama 2007](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1162/pres.16.4.337)). A fan-history essay argues that caricature kept Disney's Pirates figures out of the valley ([Park Lore](https://parklore.com/vault/uncanny-valley/)), and Cogley exaggerated his own 2024 head's proportions and motion for the same reason ([Arduino Blog](https://blog.arduino.cc/2024/12/09/this-frighteningly-realistic-animatronic-head-features-expressive-silicone-skin/)). A monster has a head start here, because nobody expects it to pass as human. The realism budget should go on the eyes.

## Karloff's trick was a dead mask with living eyes

In plain terms: the classic 1931 monster was built as a dead-looking face with very alive eyes. The makeup artist gave Karloff heavy wax eyelids because his eyes "looked too alive", and Karloff then played the monster as an innocent, mostly through his eyes and hands. Every funny or lovable Frankenstein since keeps the famous shapes and turns up the innocence and wonder. Frank can copy this: rest with heavy lids, and make every beat of feeling open them.

Jack Pierce built a high, square, flat-topped forehead with a fringe, a heavy brow, a sunken cheek (Karloff's dental bridge was removed on one side), thin dead lips, a scar, glued-on neck electrodes and **heavy mortician's-wax eyelids, added after Karloff said his eyes "look too alive"**. Under them, something "very much conscious and alive" still showed ([Creative Nonfiction](https://creativenonfiction.org/writing/the-makeup-of-a-monster/)); accounts differ on whether the lids were Karloff's idea or Pierce's ([TCM](https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/person/152336%7C76063/Jack-Pierce)). Karloff said, "Whale and I both saw the character as an innocent one. Within the heavy restrictions of my makeup, I tried to play it that way" ([MeTV](https://metv.com/stories/boris-karlof-had-a-theory-about-why-audiences-loved-his-monster), original source not named), and the BFI tells viewers to watch his eyes and hands ([BFI](https://www.bfi.org.uk/lists/boris-karloff-10-essential-films)). Later versions push the same lever. Peter Boyle's *Young Frankenstein* monster is "an almost entirely physical performance, but his facial expressions–even exaggerated–are ideal" ([The Stop Button](https://thestopbutton.com/2010/07/07/young-frankenstein-1974-mel-brooks/)), and TIME calls him the funniest screen monster ([TIME](https://entertainment.time.com/2014/01/28/frankenstein-monsters-list/slide/peter-boyle/)). The *Hotel Transylvania* team wanted Frank "fresh and new while retaining the iconic elements" ([Empire](https://empireonline.com/movies/features/hotel-transylvania-characters)). For Guillermo del Toro's 2025 creature, designer Mike Hill made the eyes deliberately unequal, a big eye for the monstrous side and a smaller eye for the human side, and Jacob Elordi said the design makes you "focus on the Creature's eyes, which is where his soul is", with "all the scars and the brow point[ing] toward my expression" ([Netflix Tudum](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/features/jacob-elordi-mike-hill-frankenstein)).

The current professional Frankenstein figures are tuned for scares. Universal calls its 2025 Epic Universe monsters "some of the most visceral, powerful, and fast-moving animated figures ever created" ([Fangoria](https://www.fangoria.com/dark-universe-new-animatronics/)), and the figure there with a projected, expressive face is Dr. Victoria Frankenstein, not the Monster (search summary) ([WDWNT](https://wdwnt.com/2025/04/first-look-at-victoria-frankenstein-animatronic-in-monsters-unchained-queue-at-epic-universe/)). Consumer props reduce the monster to a moving head and mouth with LED eyes (search summary) ([Home Depot listing](https://www.homedepot.com/p/327211567)). The research found **no commercial Frankenstein face with real expressive range**, so a head with split brows, four lids and a lip-synced mouth that reacts to conversation would stand out.

Several design consequences follow, all judgement calls. The neutral pose should be Pierce's: lids parked low and heavy, brows level and slightly low, so wonder, delight and attention become large changes from a heavy baseline, which gives the most readable contrast per degree of servo travel. The heavy lids also hide part of the EyeMech's oversized 32 mm eyeballs, which sit 75 mm apart against a human's roughly 24 mm and 63 mm ([PLAN](../docs/PLAN.md#L52)). Glowing LED eyes trade "alive" for "spooky", the opposite of the Karloff and del Toro principle, so Frank's eyes should be realistic, wet-looking and focused. Asymmetry is canon (Karloff's single sunken cheek, Hill's unequal eyes), so a slightly uneven neutral breaks robotic mirror symmetry and stays on model. Karloff opposed letting the monster speak because he feared it would lose sympathy (search summary) ([Zombo's Closet](https://www.zomboscloset.com/?p=3170)); Frank talks, so his face has to supply the innocence, with earnest, slightly slow, wondering reactions, delighted double takes and comic "processing" holds in Boyle's exaggerated spirit. An occasional mock-scare (brows slammed down, lids wide, jaw dropped fast) borrows Universal's speed for a gag without changing the character.

The project made one choice that departs from the canon. Decision D13 chose "a normally proportioned head, not the traditional campy look", with the note that "the style may change later" ([PLAN D13](../docs/PLAN.md#L387)), and Cogley's mouth sits 71 mm below the eyes where a normal face would be about 85 mm, "a little cartoonish" ([PLAN](../docs/PLAN.md#L364)). That slight cartooning is an asset against the uncanny valley. Since the skin is already cast, the Pierce silhouette is best recovered above the skin, with a flat-topped hair piece over Cogley's helmet shell, the fringe, a scar and neck electrodes. A fringe that swings slightly also adds the free secondary motion Hughes describes.

## Skin stiffness, not servo torque, sets the ceiling on Frank's expressions

In plain terms: the skin decides how much of each servo's motion reaches the surface. Professionals plan where the skin is thick or thin, where it is pinned, where it can slide, and where it should fold. Frank's skin was cast before any of that was planned, and the silicone chosen is stiffer in stretch than the silicones most hobby faces use. The single most useful thing to learn next is how hard the skin really is to stretch.

**Material.** Smooth-On lists Dragon Skin FX-Pro at Shore 2A, 288 psi tensile, 763 % elongation and 61 pli tear, against Ecoflex 00-30's 900 % and 38 pli, so FX-Pro is **1.6 times as tear-resistant**, which matters because anchors and mouth corners fail by tearing ([Smooth-On FX-Pro](https://www.smooth-on.com/products/dragon-skin-fx-pro/); [Ecoflex 00-30](https://www.smooth-on.com/products/ecoflex-00-30/)). Its listed 100 % modulus of **37.8 psi looks odd**: it is higher than Dragon Skin 10's 22 psi, a 10A rubber, and nearly four times Ecoflex 00-30's 10 psi. Taken at face value, a neo-Hookean estimate (the notes' arithmetic) gives about 7.5 N to stretch a 20 × 5 mm band by 20 %, against about 2 N for Ecoflex 00-30. An MG90S stalls at 1.8 kgf·cm at 4.8 V, about **17.7 N at a 10 mm horn**, and a working load of about a third of stall, around 6 N, is the usual rule for life and quiet ([TowerPro MG90S](https://www.towerpro.com.tw/product/mg90s-3/)). Eva's builders found that even a 2 mm Ecoflex 00-30 mask made the jaw open "much smaller than expected" ([HardwareX](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9041256)). If Frank's skin really is 5 mm of FX-Pro (`SKIN_T = 5.0` is an assumption in [params.py](../cad/params.py#L110)) and the listed modulus is right, it needs roughly nine times the force per unit width of Eva's skin. That multiplier is my estimate from two uncertain numbers, which is why the strip test in the test plan comes first. Slacker softens FX-Pro to about Shore 00-20 at 50 parts per 100 of Part B, but Smooth-On publishes no tear data with it and higher doses turn tacky ([Smooth-On Slacker](https://www.smooth-on.com/products/slacker/)). Silicone Thinner cuts tear strength in proportion to dose and should not be used as a softener ([Smooth-On Silicone Thinner](https://www.smooth-on.com/products/silicone-thinner/)).

**Thickness is the main design lever.** Disney's Physical Face Cloning work optimised only the skin's thickness, changed only through its inner surface, and its patent **lets thickness decrease but never increase**, holding it constant near attachment links ([US9082222B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9082222)). Disney's skin patents put the visible skin under about 3.2 mm, typically within 2–10 mm overall, with a soft foam or gel backing so the region around a drive point moves as a unit ([US7740953B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7740953); [US20130280493A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20130280493A1/en)). Other Disney patents cut grooves into the inner surface to make bending easier in a chosen direction, and relief cuts that pre-weaken a region ([US10137616B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10137616); [US10118320B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10118320)). The plate-theory reason is that bending stiffness scales with thickness cubed while stretch stiffness scales linearly, so halving the thickness along a line makes it eight times easier to bend there but only twice as easy to stretch. A uniformly thick skin therefore stretches like a rubber mask instead of folding. A Stan Winston School moderator sums up the trade: "Too thin and you will see the pressure points of the movement points underneath. Too thick and the servo's will not have the strength to move the skin" ([SWS forum](https://forums.stanwinstonschool.com/discussion/comment/8954/)).

**Anchors.** Every serious source says never to pull silicone at a point. Disney's integral-actuation-point patent explains that point forces move the skin "only at the point of attachment" and that glued connectors detach or tear. Its remedy is a pad of firmer rubber (30–50 durometer) with tapered wings and a harder socket that a ball-ended actuator snaps into "with tactile and audible feedback", plus separate locking pads that fix the skin to the shell, notably at the mouth corner, a common crack site ([US8568642B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8568642)). Spandex mesh embedded 1.6–4.8 mm deep is "less likely to pull out" ([US20130280493A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20130280493A1/en)). Hanson's patent adds a semi-rigid crescent along the smile crease and curved bands in the eyelid skin ([US7113848B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7113848B2/en)). The University of Tokyo's 2024 "perforation-type anchors" let the skin material fill V-shaped holes through the structure underneath, so the anchors sit flush ([University of Tokyo](https://www.u-tokyo.ac.jp/focus/en/press/z0508_00360.html)), which is a printable, drill-free idea that Frank's keyed eyelids (D27) already use. In hobby practice, Chris Ellerby of the Stan Winston School attaches cables to "small fabric pads that glue to your silicone skin with silicone adhesive" and warns that embedded magnets stress the skin and discolour it by attracting metal particles, and that closely spaced lip magnets snap to each other ([SWS forum](https://forums.stanwinstonschool.com/discussion/comment/13465/)). Sil-Poxy bonds silicone to silicone and to fabric but makes no claim for printed plastics ([Smooth-On Sil-Poxy](https://www.smooth-on.com/products/sil-poxy/)), and platinum silicone has no bond to PLA ([PLAN D27](../docs/PLAN.md#L403)), so skin-to-print joints need a mechanical key. Where anchors go also decides where folds form: a fold appears where moving skin is pushed against skin that cannot move.

**Sliding and settling.** Osaka's Affetto measurements found that changing contact between skin and shell causes hysteresis, so realised motion lagged the commanded motion, and the authors recommend attaching skin "smoothly and elastically" ([Ishihara, Wu & Asada 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2018.00119/full)). Their later work found android skin moves along nearly straight lines around the eyes and forehead where human skin follows curves ([Ishihara, Iwanaga & Asada 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8019968)). Ecoflex softens after its first load cycles (the Mullins effect), so a new skin should be cycled before final limits are set ([Swansea University](https://cronfa.swan.ac.uk/Record/cronfa57203)). Psycho Paint stretches 1,000 % with the skin but adheres best within two days of demolding and only to surfaces cleaned of release ([Smooth-On Psycho Paint](https://www.smooth-on.com/products/psycho-paint/)); Frank's skin is past that window, so a cleaned test coupon comes first.

**Mechanisms and actuators.** Rigid linkages suit motions that need precision and push-pull (jaw, lips, eyes, lids), and strings or Bowden cables suit pulls against the skin's own elasticity in tight spaces. The Morpheus head (RSS 2025) uses linkages for eyes and mouth and strings for the nose and cheeks "for subtle microexpressions" ([Morpheus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16645)). A Bowden cable only pulls, so it needs a return from the skin, a spring or a second cable, and friction and backlash grow with bends (search summaries) ([Ljubljana](https://repozitorij.uni-lj.si/IzpisGradiva.php?id=119456); [Sant'Anna](https://iris.santannapisa.it/handle/11382/532273)). Speed is less of a problem than force. At 0.10 s per 60° at 4.8 V, an MG90S closes Frank's lids (35–64° of servo travel in the saved poses, [eye_poses.json](../firmware/eye_poses.json)) in about 60–107 ms with no load, against a human closing phase of about 75–100 ms ([Wikipedia: Blinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blinking)). Its roughly 600°/s beats the 300–350°/s peak of a 15° human saccade ([Wikipedia: Saccade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccade)). Noise is the bigger weakness: Eva's builders found servo noise "significant and distracting" ([HardwareX](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9041256)), and a holding servo hums because it is fighting its load ([Arduino forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/quitening-the-servo-motors/27030)). The common quiet upgrade, the coreless KST X08, is **slower than an MG90S at 6 V (0.15 against 0.08 s per 60°)** and only reaches 0.09 s at 8.4 V ([KST X08 spec](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0570/1766/3541/files/X08_V6.0_Technical_Specifcation.pdf)). Micro linear actuators take 0.7–2 s for a 20 mm stroke ([Actuonix PQ12](https://cdn.robotshop.com/media/f/fir/rb-fir-42/pdf/actuonix-pq12-datasheet.pdf)) and shape-memory wire about a second to contract and longer to cool ([Flexinol data](https://cdn.robotshop.com/media/d/dyn/rb-dyn-01/pdf/flexinol-technical-data.pdf)), both far too slow for speech. Some "metal gear" MG90S clones hide a plastic first-stage pinion that strips, so it is worth opening one of each batch ([TuyaOpen forum](https://forum-tuyaopen.discourse.group/t/mg90s/24)).

## Frank today has the hardware and a new engine, but nothing is tuned on the skinned head

In plain terms: Frank's head can already make most of the faces his character bible asks for, and as of 2026-10-30 a new face engine moves his eyes, lids, brows and mouth corners with his feelings. But the engine was tuned on a drawing of the face, the skin is not yet attached to the moving parts, and nobody has measured how far anything actually moves. Some of the research notes describe older versions of the head or the software; where they disagree with the project files, this report follows the files.

The mechanical base is Cogley's whole head, built as designed (D14, D17), with his four-servo TPU brows (D15) and the printed EyeMech ε3.2 set ([PLAN](../docs/PLAN.md#L388)). Its fitted motions are eye pan and tilt (one servo each for both eyes), four independent lids, four brow ends, a jaw on two mirrored MG996Rs, four lips and four corners. There is no neck ([PLAN](../docs/PLAN.md#L69)), no tongue servo (`TONGUE = False`, [mouth.py](../frank/mouth.py#L90)) and no cheek actuator, since the `CheekBone` is a static band of the frame and Cogley's "cheek" servos are the mouth corners. On the strong side, four independent lids allow winks, squints and lower-lid raises, two servos per corner allow up, down and wide, and two servos per brow separate the worried brow from the surprised arch. Those are more than most hobby faces have. On the weak side, the brows' downward travel "barely showed" at Cogley's 10°, the team's 35/20° travel and the direction table are unchecked, and the left outer brow servo was faulty ([BUILD_LOG](../docs/BUILD_LOG.md#L122)). "The lips and corners hardly showed with his angles" ([BUILD_LOG](../docs/BUILD_LOG.md#L112)). The board program clamps every mouth servo to "the range his poses use" ([cogley_mouth_main.py](../firmware/cogley_mouth_main.py#L68)). A lower lid servo died on 2026-10-30, and the horns sit differently on their splines, so equal commands give unequal faces ([BUILD_LOG](../docs/BUILD_LOG.md#L203)). Each millimetre of silicone on a lash edge costs about 3° of blink ([PRINT_GUIDE](../docs/PRINT_GUIDE.md#L11)). No Cogley mechanism has documented hard stops, so the only limits are software clamps, and both the old idle animation and the new engine can command eye tilt down to about 47° when the slider's floor is 60° (±15° about a centre of 62°; an inference from [eye_anim.py](../firmware/eye_anim.py#L20), [rig.json](../frank/engine/rig.json) and [eye_poses.json](../firmware/eye_poses.json)).

On the software side, the face performance engine in `frank/engine/` went in on 2026-10-30 and runs inside `frank/chat.py` and `frank/web.py`, switched off with `--no-face` ([FACE_ENGINE.md](../docs/FACE_ENGINE.md)); the spec's own status table still says "building". It follows the earlier report's design. An orchestrator with eight states fires each tag when its words reach the speaker and picks a gaze target from the camera. A synthesiser keeps a slow mood and ten fading tag weights and blends them region by region, with asymmetry kept as a separate term so it survives the blend. An animator adds blinks, eye-eye-nose saccades around the target, gaze aversion, idle drift and speech brow beats, then smooths every channel through a critically damped spring. It sends the eye-board servos through `Mouth.link()` and the mouth's emotion part (smile, one-sided smile, and a jaw floor for surprise) through the new `Mouth.expression()`, while `mouth.py` still owns the jaw and lips from the audio ([mouth.py](../frank/mouth.py#L244)). Several of its settings are places for tuning rather than gaps. The overall expressivity `gain` is 0.8, deliberate restraint taken from the bible's "not every movement at once", with per-tag factors from 0.9 to 1.25 ([synth.py](../frank/engine/synth.py#L39)). The upper lids rest at 0.85 of open ([channels.py](../frank/engine/channels.py#L18)). The gaze maps 30° of a visitor's yaw onto 30° of servo and 20° of pitch onto 15°, start values that assume an eye linkage nobody has measured ([animator.py](../frank/engine/animator.py#L35)). The only angle limit is a global −27..207° clamp plus the saved poses ([rig.json](../frank/engine/rig.json)). The spec lists exact tag timing from ElevenLabs timestamps, sending the jaw early by the measured servo lag, MediaPipe puppeteering for recorded clips, a Pico-side fallback idle, and "tuning on the head with the skin on" as later work, and nobody has yet reported running its first-run checklist (curious must raise both brows, skeptical Frank's own left one). The UART link carried jaw, eyes, lids and brows together for 30 s on 2026-10-29 ([BUILD_LOG](../docs/BUILD_LOG.md#L66)), so every face servo is reachable from the laptop.

The constraints on change are physical. The skin's inner surface keeps at least 3 mm off Cogley's parts, so every anchor needs a standoff to reach it. Clearances between modules are 2.7–8.2 mm. Each board brings out 12 servo sockets (GP4–GP15), and **only TON and EXA are free on each of the two boards, four sockets in all** ([BOM](../docs/BOM.md#L14); [Head Power Wiring](https://claude.ai/artifact/NNjtnRuZohye1SKmWCQ5Gw)). The buck is rated 15 A continuous and each board has a 10 A fuse. Every lead and the fan's air share one hole of about 530 mm² and a Ø25 bore through the neck ([params.py](../cad/params.py#L591)). The canister's window sheet sits 19 mm from the face ([params.py](../cad/params.py#L471)). EyeMech is licensed CC BY-NC-SA, so modifying it makes a share-alike derivative ([PLAN](../docs/PLAN.md#L89)). The maker has two printers and a soldering iron and no drill, so every joint must be printed with its holes, clamped, glued, or fitted with heat-set inserts.

## Recommendations ranked by effort

In plain terms: start with what costs nothing. Frank's 20 servos can already make most of the faces his bible describes, and the new engine now tells them what to do; what it needs is checking and tuning on the real, skinned head. Next, attach the skin properly and measure it, because that decides how much of the servo motion shows. Spend the four free sockets only once the skinned face shows what software alone cannot do, and leave the neck and a second skin for the next version.

If only five things get done, they should be these: run the engine's first-run checks on the head and calibrate its gaze (A1, A2), set a heavier-lidded neutral and more squint (A3, A4), finish and repair the brows (B1), anchor the skin at the moving points (C1), and run the stiffness strip and dot-grid tests (T1, T4). The first group needs no parts, so it is the only group that can happen before Halloween. The cost columns assume MG90S stall at 0.7–0.86 A ([Tinytronics datasheet](https://www.tinytronics.nl/product_files/000263_Data_Sheet_of_MG90S_Analog_Servo_Motor.pdf)) and MG996R stall at 1.4 A ([TowerPro MG996R](https://www.towerpro.com.tw/product/mg996r/)).

### Four free sockets can buy a sneer and a cheek lift, once software shows the need

### Skin, anchors and eyes decide how much of the motion reaches the surface

This group is cast, glue and print work with no new channels. The skin is cured and has no anchors, so pads have to be bonded to its inner face with Sil-Poxy and reach the mechanism through printed standoffs that bridge the 3 mm gap. Heat-set inserts set with the soldering iron and keyed or clamped joints avoid any drilling.

## Six bench tests settle the numbers nobody has measured

In plain terms: almost every number that decides what to build next is a guess today, including how stiff the skin is, how hard a pad can be pulled before it tears, how much force a servo really gives, and where on the face the motion dies. Each of these tests takes an afternoon, needs no drill, and decides one of the recommendations above.

| # | Test | Question it answers | How (no drill) | What decides |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Skin stiffness strip | Is FX-Pro's listed 37.8 psi modulus right, and how much force does each zone need? | Cut a 100 × 20 mm strip from trimmed flash (or pour one 5 mm thick), measure its thickness, clamp one end in a printed clamp, hang masses from 100 g to 1 kg in steps, and measure the stretch; repeat with FX-Pro plus 50 parts Slacker | If a zone needs well over about 6 N at the horn radius used, thin it (C4), soften it, or plan D1 |
| T2 | Snap-pad pull-out | Will an anchor carry servo force without tearing the skin? | Bond pads (with and without mesh, with a printed ball stud) to 5 mm coupons, hang increasing weights until a pad pulls out or the skin tears, and repeat in peel for a keyed-lid coupon (Q22) | A pad should outlast the servo's stall force at its horn (about 18 N at 10 mm), or the soft limits must keep the pull below the measured hold |
| T3 | Servo force, stops and travel | What does each servo deliver, where are its real stops, how far does the surface move with skin on, and how long is the lag? | A luggage or kitchen scale on a horn at a known radius; drive each mechanism slowly to binding and note the angle; measure jaw opening and brow travel in mm with the skin on; film a blink and a jaw step against a click at 240 fps on a phone | The soft-limit table (A8); wider mouth ranges (A6); whether blinks reach human speed; how early to command the jaw for lip-sync |
| T4 | Dot-grid filming | Where does the motion die, dimple, or fold in the wrong place? | Draw a 10 mm dot grid on the skin with a silicone-safe marker; film each servo alone at its limits from the front and side; film Lars making the same face | Where dots barely move, enlarge the pad or thin the skin; where the skin dimples, add backing or a larger tapered pad; where folds form wrong, add a locking line or a groove (C2, C4); B3 and B4 go ahead only if their stand-ins fail here |
| T5 | Cycle and drift | Does the face return to rest, and does it survive use? | 100 brow cycles and several hundred corner cycles with the skin on, photographing the rest pose before and after; pre-cycle the whole skin through every pose a few dozen times before setting final limits; run the head for 1–3 hours and note any hot servo (Eva's runs stopped at 3 hours) | Re-tune the rest poses; add powder or clearance (C3); add rest periods if servos run hot |
| T6 | Quick read test (optional, my addition) | Do the ten tag faces read as intended? | Show each tag face to a few people and ask them to name it; note the confusions | Raise the gain or change the pose where faces are confused |

## Conclusion

The usual hobby question, how many servos a face needs, turns out to be the wrong one for Frank. His count already matches what Disney spends on a showpiece face and what the 2026 research heads achieve on 9 g servos. The limits sit on either side of the servos. Above them, the new engine now moves the upper face, which the perception research says is exactly the part that keeps a realistic male face out of the creepy zone, but it does so at 80 % strength with ranges set on sliders and a drawing, so tuning it against the skinned head is the cheapest gain left. Below them, an unanchored skin of unknown stiffness decides how much of each motion reaches the audience, and the two well-documented ways to lose expression on a physical face, low intensity on Nikola and weaker-than-preview faces on Ameca, both live in that layer. This also fixes the order of work. The four spare sockets should be spent after the dot-grid film shows which software stand-ins fail, not before, because a well-coupled lower lid or a one-sided upper-lip raise may already deliver the smile and the sneer.

The character fits the hardware better than it first appears. Karloff's performance was a heavy, nearly dead mask with living eyes, and Frank's richest hardware is in precisely those places: four independent lids, split brows and a camera that knows where everyone is. A heavy-lidded neutral that opens for every beat of interest gives large, readable changes from small servo travel, hides the oversized eyeballs, and stays on model, while deliberate asymmetry and Boyle-style exaggeration turn the monster's stiffness into comedy. The parts of this plan no source has tested are the perceptual ones: how much gain the skin eats, whether stand-ins read as the real motions, and whether asymmetry adds as much life as it seems to. Those answers will come from filming the head, not from more reading.
