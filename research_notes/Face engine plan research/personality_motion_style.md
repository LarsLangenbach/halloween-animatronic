# Personality through motion style: frameworks, findings and a parameter set for Frank

Scope: how a character's personality shows in *how* the face moves (timing, amplitude, symmetry, gaze habits, blink, restraint), not in which expression it picks, so that a face engine can carry personality as tunable numbers. Researched 2026-10-30. Several primary PDFs were downloaded and their text extracted locally to get exact numbers (Neff 2010, Gielniak & Thomaz 2012, Takayama et al. 2011, Andrist et al. 2015, Durupinar et al. PERFORM, Disney Research 2020, Maas's Vector reference manual); where only an abstract or a secondary summary was reachable, that is said.

## 1. Laban Movement Analysis (Effort) in agent and robot motion, and how Effort changes perceived emotion and personality

### Takeaway
Laban Effort gives four bipolar "how" axes (Space: Direct/Indirect, Weight: Strong/Light, Time: Sudden/Sustained, Flow: Bound/Free). EMOTE turned them into low-level animation parameters, and later work regressed personality traits onto them. The most useful single result for Frank is the PERFORM study: extraversion, neuroticism and openness load mainly on Time, Flow and Space, so a reserved, unflappable and skeptical character reads as Direct, Sustained and Bound, and curiosity reads as Indirect and Free. Effort qualities stay legible even on 2 to 4 DOF robot heads.

### Cited Findings
- EMOTE (Expressive MOTion Engine; Chi, Costa, Zhao, Badler, SIGGRAPH 2000, pp. 173-182) applies Laban Effort and Shape parameters to keyframed arm and torso motion that was authored separately, so expression is a modifier layer over a base motion and not a separate animation. — [SIGGRAPH history archive](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=118266); [Penn repository record](https://repository.upenn.edu/hms/48); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/344779.352172)
- Effort definitions used in later work: Space (Indirect vs Direct) is attention to the environment; Weight (Light vs Strong) is the sense of impact of a movement; Time (Sustained vs Sudden) is the attitude toward time, with or without urgency; Flow (Free vs Bound) is the degree of control. Each factor runs between an "indulging" pole (Indirect, Light, Sustained, Free) and a "condensing" pole (Direct, Strong, Sudden, Bound). Using all Effort elements at once is uncommon; people use them in combinations of 2 ("States") or 3 ("Drives"), and there are 32 Drive constellations. — [Durupinar, Kapadia, Deutsch, Neff, Badler, "PERFORM: Perceptual Approach for Adding OCEAN Personality to Human Motion using Laban Movement Analysis", ACM TOG](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/PERFORM_TOG.pdf)
- PERFORM's low-level parameter set (built on EMOTE, quantized by certified movement analysts, then fitted by multivariate linear regression on Effort values in [-1, 1]) includes: animation speed, anticipation velocity and time, overshoot velocity and time, inflection time, a time exponent that magnifies acceleration or deceleration, spline tension and continuity, wrist bend/extension/twist/frequency, elbow twist/displacement/frequency, torso rotation magnitude and frequency, head rotation magnitude and frequency, breathing squash magnitude and frequency, and Shape coefficients (enclosing/spreading, sinking/rising, retreating/advancing). Selected coefficients (intercept; Space, Weight, Time, Flow): animation speed 0.558; 0, 0.001, **0.470**, 0.001. Overshoot velocity 0.344; -0.042, -0.042, 0, **-0.458**. Anticipation velocity 0.223; -0.011, **0.297**, 0, -0.029. Head rotation magnitude 1.210; **-0.804**, 0.008, 0.004, -0.178. Head rotation frequency 1.078; **-1.225**, 0.104, -0.017, 0.184. Torso rotation frequency 1.283; -0.179, 0.223, 0.067, **-1.410**. Breathing frequency 0.687; -0.031, 0.263, -0.156, -0.188. In words: Time drives overall speed; Flow drives overshoot and the amount of secondary oscillation; Space drives how much and how often the head looks around; Weight drives anticipation. (Sign convention inferred from the coefficients: negative = indulging pole, e.g. Indirect gives more head rotation and Free gives more overshoot. One worked example in the paper's text appears to contradict this for Weight, so check before reuse.) — [PERFORM, Table IV](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/PERFORM_TOG.pdf)
- PERFORM personality-to-Effort correlations from a Mechanical Turk study (244 unique participants, at least 30 answers per question, Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate below 0.069): Openness high = Indirect + Free (low = Direct + Bound). Conscientiousness high = Direct + Sustained + Bound. Extraversion high = Indirect + Sudden + Free (low = Direct + Sustained + Bound). Agreeableness high = Light + Sustained (low = Strong + Sudden; participants read Sudden motion as rude). Neuroticism high = Indirect + Sudden + Free (low = Direct + Sustained + Bound). "Time has the highest correlation with Extroversion among all personality factors." — [PERFORM, Table II and §6](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/PERFORM_TOG.pdf)
- PERFORM validation (55 participants, 30 per question, 3 models x 3 actions): the share of viewers who picked the intended pole was 93.4% for extraversion, 90.8% neuroticism, 89.4% conscientiousness, 74.5% openness and 74.2% agreeableness (all p < 0.001). Extraversion and neuroticism are the most readable from motion; agreeableness and openness are the hardest. — [PERFORM §7.2](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/PERFORM_TOG.pdf)
- PERFORM cites North (1972) and Bartenieff (1980): a rich vocabulary of movement qualities indicates a more stable and social personality, and less Effort goes with less expressivity and more psychological distress. — [PERFORM §2](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/PERFORM_TOG.pdf)
- Knight & Simmons (ICRA 2016) put all four Efforts onto a 2-DOF Nao head and a 4-DOF Keepon. In an online survey with 1,028 ratings, all four Efforts were legible with statistical significance, without human-like degrees of freedom. — [CMU RI](https://ri.cmu.edu/?p=122282); [CMU RI publication page](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/publications/laban-head-motions-convey-robot-state-a-call-for-robot-body-language/)
- In Knight's operationalization, Sudden Time = sharper accelerations and higher speed, and Sustained = slower acceleration and lower speed. — [Knight, "Expressive Motion for Low Degree-of-Freedom Robots" (CMU thesis)](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/publications/expressive-motion-low-degree-freedom-robots)
- Knight & Simmons argue that motion quality layered on task motion ("the how") conveys attitudes such as confidence, adherence to a deadline or flexibility of attention. Effort features can be layered on the same joints that do the task. — [HRI 2015 "Layering Laban effort features on robot task motions"](https://ri.cmu.edu/?p=122339); [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303886000_Laban_head-motions_convey_robot_state_A_call_for_robot_body_language)
- In a CoBot field deployment, bystanders used motion characteristics (e.g. appearing rushed) to read the robot's state, and this changed whether they interrupted it. — [Oregon State colloquium abstract, Knight](https://engineering.oregonstate.edu/colloquium/algorithms-charismatic-robots)

### Inferences
- Effort maps cleanly onto Frank's channels even without a neck: Time sets onset/release durations and acceleration profile; Flow sets overshoot, settle oscillation and secondary micro-motion; Space sets how many gaze targets the eyes visit and how often; Weight sets anticipation depth and how hard a move "lands" (a short decisive stop vs a soft ease).
- Frank's default (reserved, unflappable, skeptical) sits near Direct + Sustained + Bound, with moderately Strong weight on the disagreeable moments (e.g. a decisive brow drop on "irritated"). His "fascinated" state flips to Indirect + Free + (somewhat) Sudden, which is the PERFORM signature of openness and extraversion. That flip is a ready-made, measurable way to make the restraint "drop".
- Because PERFORM found that Sudden motion reads as rude or disagreeable, a small dose of suddenness on skeptical or irritated beats (fast brow snap, slow everything else) is a principled way to add Frank's slight arrogance without changing amplitude.

### Gaps
- The original EMOTE paper's exact parameter formulas (inflection time, time exponent, flourishes) could not be fetched: the Penn link now redirects, the repository returned 403 and the CMU mirror failed TLS. PERFORM's Table IV, which builds on EMOTE, is used instead.
- Knight & Simmons' per-Effort effect sizes were not retrieved (the PDF host failed TLS); only the 1,028-ratings headline and fragmentary snippets are available.
- No study found that applies Laban Effort specifically to facial features (brows, lids, mouth) as opposed to head, arms or bodies.

## 2. Personality-to-motion mappings in agents and robots (Big Five / extraversion via amplitude, speed, frequency, gaze); what is measured and effect sizes

### Takeaway
Across virtual agents and robots, extraversion is the most robustly readable trait from motion, and it rides on rate (how often), amplitude (how big), speed and latency (how fast), and gaze amount. The effects are real but modest per channel: in Neff et al. 2010, gesture performance, gesture rate and language together explained 27% of the variance in perceived extraversion, and language had the largest weight. Frank's introverted-but-confident profile maps to low rate, narrow amplitude, longer latency and less mutual gaze, with the arrogance and the curiosity added through other channels.

### Cited Findings
- Neff, Wang, Abbott & Walker (IVA 2010) summarise the psychology literature in "gestural correlates of extraversion" (their Table 2). Introvert: backward lean or turning away, narrow amplitude, inward gestures and self-contact, low rate, slow speed and response time, low smoothness and rhythm disturbance. Extravert: forward lean, wide amplitude, outward and horizontal spreading gestures, high rate with more head/hand/leg movement, fast, smooth and fluent, head tilt, more eye contact. Extraverts also have shorter response latency, and "the gestures of introverts persist more". — [Neff et al. 2010 preprint](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/extraversion_final_PRE_PRINT.pdf)
- Neff et al.'s parameter settings for max introversion vs max extraversion (their Table 3): stroke scale x*0.5, y*0.6, z*0.8 vs x*1.4, y*1.2, z*1.1; stroke position x-12 cm, y-5 cm vs x+12 cm, y+10 cm; duration 100% vs 80%; collar bones down 5 deg vs up 10 deg; spine forward rotation -6 vs +6 deg; centre-of-mass shift -5 vs +6 cm; body motion scale 10-60% vs 100%. The high gesture rate used about twice as many gestures as the low. — [Neff et al. 2010](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/extraversion_final_PRE_PRINT.pdf)
- Results (40 web subjects; TIPI extraversion ratings on a 1-7 scale): gesture performance F = 3.5, df = 3, p = .015; gesture rate F = 17.1, df = 1, p < .001; linguistic extraversion F = 55.5, df = 1, p < .001. Standardised regression: perceived extraversion = .09*Performance + .12*Rate + .21*Language, R^2 = 27%. Misaligned combinations (e.g. introverted words with extraverted gestures) were rated as natural as aligned ones; the movement parameters did not affect naturalness. — [Neff et al. 2010](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/extraversion_final_PRE_PRINT.pdf)
- Craenen et al. (2018) built 45 Pepper gestures by varying amplitude and speed of five stock animations. Thirty observers rated Big-Five traits and Godspeed. There was a significant association between amplitude/speed and assigned personality, significant for extraversion and present for all core gestures except "Disengaging"; amplitude and speed were also associated with Godspeed scores. — [Craenen et al., HRI 2018 workshop paper](https://bcraenen.home.xs4all.nl/publications/hri2018wcpgw.pdf); [Glasgow MuMMER RO-MAN 2018 paper](https://www.psy.gla.ac.uk/~amold/papers/2018/mummer-ro-man-51.pdf)
- A 2023 IJSR paper citing Deshmukh reports that higher gesture amplitude and speed are associated with higher perceived extraversion **and** neuroticism. — [Springer IJSR 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12369-023-01051-8)
- Lee, Peng, Jin & Yan (J. Communication 2006; AIBO; 2x2 between-subjects, N = 48) found that people accurately recognised an introverted vs extraverted robot from verbal and nonverbal behaviour; the extraverted AIBO had more colourful and more frequently changing lights and more exaggerated motion. People preferred a robot whose personality *complemented* their own, mediated by social presence. — [DeepDyve record](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/doc/KCcB700RVH); behavioural details from a secondary course summary: [Lehigh COMM298 notes](https://wordpress.lehigh.edu/comm298-398-2022/?p=1245)
- Andrist, Mutlu & Tapus (CHI 2015) expressed robot extraversion or introversion **through gaze alone**. Online validation (22 participants after exclusions): extraversion rating M = 3.79 (SD 0.54) for extravert gaze vs M = 3.53 (SD 0.59) for introvert gaze, F(1, 84) = 5.09, p = .027, a small difference. Lab study (40 participants): a significant personality-matching interaction on time-on-task, F(1, 35) = 14.80, p < .001; extraverts persisted longer with the extraverted robot (p = .039) and introverts with the introverted robot (p = .010). — [Andrist et al. CHI 2015 PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2015/CHI15-Andrist.pdf)
- Chittaro & Serra (2004), as cited in PERFORM, mapped neuroticism to animation speed and extraversion to interpersonal distance. Neff et al. (2011) found non-signalling (self-adaptor) hand gestures significantly raise perceived neuroticism. — [PERFORM related work](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/PERFORM_TOG.pdf)
- Takayama, Dooley (Pixar) & Ju (HRI 2011; online video study, N = 273; PR2 animated by a Pixar animator). Showing **forethought** (anticipatory "thinking" motion before acting) made viewers more sure of their reading of the robot's intent (M = 5.69 vs 5.20, F(1, 1067) = 26.43, p < .001). It also made the robot more appealing (M = 4.83 vs 4.27, F(1, 1078) = 34.18, p < .001) and more approachable (M = 5.05 vs 4.54, F(1, 1072) = 27.05, p < .001). Showing a goal-oriented **reaction** to the outcome (disappointment on failure, happiness on success) made it seem smarter (competent + intelligent) regardless of success, F(1, 1063) = 18.40, p < .001. Numbers were decoded from a PDF with a broken font map; the direction of every effect and the p-values are unambiguous, but treat the decimals as near-certain rather than certain. — [Takayama, Dooley & Ju 2011 PDF](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Frank, the extraversion literature gives the *default* (introvert-coded) settings: low expression rate, narrow amplitude, a beat of latency before reacting, persistent holds, and lower mutual gaze. "Slightly arrogant" and "skeptical" do not come from extraversion; they come from agreeableness (Strong/Sudden accents) and from gaze dominance (section 4).
- Because each motion channel contributes only a modest share of the variance (and language contributes more), Frank's dialogue will carry much of his personality. The face engine's job is to stay consistent with the dialogue, not to carry the whole character alone. Neff's finding that misaligned verbal and nonverbal cues were not judged less natural gives some room, but it is not licence to contradict the dialogue.
- Takayama's result argues for a "considering" pre-move (eyes shift, brows micro-gather) before Frank answers a question, and for visible reactions to outcomes. Both read as intelligence, which fits an aloof intellectual.
- The AIBO complementarity result and the Andrist matching result conflict on matching vs complementing. For a theatrical show character that is not a long-term companion, this matters less than consistency.

### Gaps
- No primary-source numbers found for Kim, Kwak et al. on robot personality, for Mutlu-specific personality work beyond gaze, or for Hoffman's Shimon beyond the movement-first design argument ([Hoffman & Ju, JHRI 3(1)](https://doi.org/10.5898/JHRI.3.1.Hoffman)); full texts were not retrieved.
- Craenen et al.'s exact effect sizes were not extracted (only abstracts and snippets were read).
- Almost all personality-motion studies use arms or bodies; face-only studies of personality from motion style (as opposed to static expression) were not found.

## 3. Acting and animation craft: personality through motion, restraint, economy, "earned" big moments, deadpan archetypes

### Takeaway
The craft sources agree on one principle that suits Frank exactly: a character who rarely moves makes each small move count, and over-animating dilutes it. Gromit (brows plus a head tilt), K-2SO (an unflappable droid whose life is in eye darts) and Keaton (stillness against chaos) are the closest archetypes. The research supports the same idea from the other side: forethought (anticipation) and visible reaction make a character read as thinking. The evidence here is mostly practitioner testimony, not experiments.

### Cited Findings
- Nick Park on Gromit: he originally planned to give Gromit a mouth and growls, found it too hard, and moved the performance into the eyebrows; he describes the eyebrows "and maybe a cock of the head" as what brings Gromit to life. — [Bullseye with Jesse Thorn transcript, Nick Park](https://maximumfun.org/transcripts/bullseye-with-jesse-thorn/transcript-bullseye-with-jesse-thorn-wallace-gromit-creator); [Kottke, how Gromit is built](https://kottke.org/16/05/how-gromit-from-wallace-and-gromit-is-built)
- Aardman director Merlin Crossingham (Vengeance Most Fowl, 2024): the tendency is to over-animate Gromit, and over-animating him dilutes the power of his expression; Feathers McGraw (no pupils, no brow) is an even harder case of performance through stillness. — [Animation World Network, "the power of stillness" (mirror)](https://d1xfgk3mh635yx.cloudfront.net/animationworld/wallace-gromit-vengeance-most-fowl-power-stillness-stop-motion)
- ILM's Hal Hickel on K-2SO: the first observation about the character was that he is "unflappable", unlike the "very flappable" C-3PO. Droids "don't have mouths that smile or frown or eyebrows that move up or down", so keeping eye rotation gave "little thinking moments where the eyes are kind of bouncing around". Animators studied Alan Tudyk's footage for the "little telltale movements of the eyes as they flick from one eye line to another". Hickel credits Tudyk's comic timing, which largely carried into the final animation. — [Cartoon Brew, Hal Hickel on K-2SO](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/feature-film/ilm-animation-supervisor-hal-hickel-explains-add-personality-star-wars-droid-147069.html)
- Hickel's first K-2SO test used an audio clip of Tudyk and "the minimum of some eye blinks and eye rotations". — search-result summary of Rogue One interviews ([Slashfilm interview](https://www.slashfilm.com/550215/hal-hickel-alan-tudyk-rogue-one-interview/); [Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/rogue-one-star-wars-interview-hal-hickel-alan-tudyk/)); exact source page not confirmed.
- Ed Hooks, *Acting for Animators*: the first of his seven essential acting concepts is "thinking tends to lead to conclusions, and emotion tends to lead to action". Others cited in summaries include "scenes begin in the middle" and "a scene is a negotiation". Wording comes from secondary summaries, not the book. — [ArtStation Magazine on Hooks](https://magazine.artstation.com/2020/01/acting-for-animators-with-ed-hooks/); [student summary of the seven principles](https://adweaver.blogspot.com/2012/12/7-acting-principle-ed-hooks.html)
- Practitioner course material: overdoing a shot fails the story; "less is more" applies especially to animated acting, i.e. grounded acting without "luscious arcs and movements on every syllable". — [Lupin House Animation course page](https://www.skool.com/lupin-house-animation-6588)
- A stage-acting article reframes "less is more" as **economy**: the effectiveness of the energy a performer chooses to spend ("effort versus economy"), not simply doing less. — [Backstage, what directors mean by "less is more"](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/directors-really-mean-say-less-4820/)
- Keaton: critic Dana Stevens calls his "self-contained stillness" a "secret weapon". Commentators describe the blank face as engaging the audience to supply the emotion, with cuts between his watching face and the action; his stillness coexisted with extreme physical agility. In the early Arbuckle shorts he smiled and laughed, so the stone face was a chosen persona. — [Irish Times (Iannucci)](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/a-salute-to-comedy-s-great-stoneface-1.1289259); [UB Spectrum on John Plotz lecture](https://www.ubspectrum.com/r/72e682ad9a); secondary-quality sources, see Gaps.
- Takayama et al. (above) operationalised two classic animation principles on a robot with a Pixar animator: forethought (anticipation) and reaction. Both measurably improved how people read the robot (appeal, approachability, perceived intelligence). — [Takayama, Dooley & Ju 2011](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf)
- Disney Research's animatronic bust used the principles of secondary action (breathing, blinking, saccades in a base "alive" layer) and overlapping action (eyes reach the target first at high speed, the head follows more slowly). In its Engage layer "eyebrows are furrowed to give the character some contempt for being distracted", a personality beat delivered by one channel. — [Pan et al., "Realistic and Interactive Robot Gaze", IROS 2020 (PDF)](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/root.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Earned" big moments are a contrast effect: the rarer and smaller Frank's baseline motion, the bigger a fascinated burst will read at the same servo amplitude. The engine should therefore budget expressivity over time (a rolling allowance) and not just per tag.
- The Gromit and K-2SO lessons translate directly to Frank's hardware. The four-servo brow (inner/outer per side) is his Gromit channel and the eyes are his K-2SO channel. The mouth corners and lips should be used sparingly, because a dry character "smiles" with one corner and the eyes and not with the whole mouth.
- A "moving hold" (a held pose with tiny noise so the character does not die) is the craft answer to stillness that still reads as alive. Disney's L1 "alive" layer is the robot implementation of it.
- Hooks's "emotion leads to action" suggests Frank's thinking states (concentrating, skeptical) should look like conclusions forming (slow narrowing, a settled hold), while his emotional states (surprised, fascinated, irritated) should produce action (onsets, changes).

### Gaps
- No primary Pixar or Disney text on "personality animation" (e.g. Thomas & Johnston's *Illusion of Life* chapters, Pixar talks) was retrieved; Hooks's principles come only via secondary summaries.
- No sourced craft analysis was found for Marvin the Paranoid Android or HAL 9000 (both are performance by voice plus near-total stillness); this is a known archetype but unsourced here.
- The Keaton sources are commentary and press pieces of uneven quality, not scholarly analysis (Benayoun's *The Look of Buster Keaton* was not retrieved).

## 4. Gaze and personality: aversion patterns, dominance, skeptical looks, eyelid level as a signal

### Takeaway
Gaze is the cheapest and strongest personality channel Frank has. Amount of mutual gaze signals extraversion and friendliness (about 50% reads friendliest; 100% reads less friendly). The *pattern* of gaze signals power (looking more while speaking than while listening reads as dominant). Well-timed aversions read as thinking. Eyelid aperture reads as alertness and intelligence (lower lids look tired and less intelligent), so a skeptical half-lid must stay on the alert side of tired.

### Cited Findings
- Andrist, Mutlu & Tapus (CHI 2015) measured human dyads and turned the data into robot gaze models (mean, SD of fixation lengths in seconds). Extraverted instructors fixated the partner 2.66 s (SD 0.80) in-task and 3.91 s (1.22) between tasks, and the puzzle 4.04 s (2.12) in-task and 1.01 s (1.26) between tasks. Introverted instructors fixated the partner 0.57 s (0.19) in-task and 1.59 s (0.39) between tasks, and the puzzle 11.65 s (11.17) in-task and 6.21 s (8.14) between tasks. The robot drew gaze lengths from normal distributions with these parameters. Extraverts engage in significantly more mutual gaze. — [Andrist et al. 2015, Table 2](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2015/CHI15-Andrist.pdf)
- Conversational gaze aversion (Andrist, Mutlu, Gleicher 2013, from 24 dyadic conversations coded for length, timing and frequency of aversions relative to speech): agents using these aversions were perceived as thinking, elicited more disclosure and managed turn-taking better. The 2014 HRI version ported this to a NAO. — [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=557586); [Wisconsin People and Robots lab](https://peopleandrobots.wisc.edu/publications/conversational-gaze-aversion-for-virtual-agents-inproceedings/)
- A 2023 Frontiers study varying a humanoid's gaze-aversion ratio: a low aversion ratio shortened interactions, and people partly mirrored the robot's gaze. A 2024 SemDial paper: random or poorly timed robot gaze aversion disrupts conversational fluency and reduces naturalness. — [Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2023](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2023.1062714/text); [SemDial 2024](https://www.semdial.org/anthology/Z24-Somashekarappa_semdial_0037.pdf)
- Fukayama et al. (CHI 2002) showed that gaze alone lets an interface agent manage users' impressions. Per Koda et al.'s later description, the gaze amounts tested were 25/50/75/100%, with 50% rated most friendly and friendliness dropping at full gaze (Japanese evaluators). — [CHI 2002 record](https://bibtex.github.io/CHI-2002-FukayamaOMSH.html); [Koda et al., ICAART 2017](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2017/62294/62294.pdf)
- Visual Dominance Ratio (Dovidio & Ellyson 1982, *Social Psychology Quarterly* 45(2)): the ratio of looking-while-speaking to looking-while-listening. High-power people look relatively more while speaking; low-power people look more while listening. Observers decode these patterns reliably. — [Wikipedia: Visual Dominance Ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Dominance_Ratio)
- Saccade statistics for agents (Lee, Badler & Badler, "Eyes Alive", SIGGRAPH 2002): the model covers magnitude, direction, duration, velocity and inter-saccadic interval. Duration is roughly D = D0 + d*A with d of about 2-2.7 ms/deg and D0 of about 20-30 ms (as restated by later work); saccadic reaction time averages 180-220 ms; the minimum inter-saccadic interval is 50-100 ms; natural saccades rarely exceed 15 deg. SmartBody's defaults, derived from this paper, cap magnitude at 10 deg for listening and 12 deg for talking and thinking. — [Eyes Alive PDF (Penn)](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~badler/papers/EyesAlive.pdf); [SmartBody saccade configuration](https://smartbody.ict.usc.edu/HTML/documentation/SB/Configuring-Eye-Saccades_20709915.html)
- Disney Research animatronic: during mutual gaze the eyes saccade randomly between three points on the person (two eyes and the nose) with random timing of 0.1-0.5 s; eyelid motion bandwidth was 10 Hz vs at most 5 Hz for the head and neck; the eyes always lead head turns. The authors found that saccades "appear to significantly improve character realism". — [Pan et al. 2020 PDF](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/root.pdf)
- Eyelids: in a digitally altered-photo study (American Society of Plastic Surgeons; small, 20 raters, reported via press), upper-eyelid droop was the strongest single cue for looking tired, and eyebrow shape mattered more than absolute brow position for perceived mood. — [ScienceDaily 2008](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/05/080528102900.htm)
- Talamas et al. (J. Exp. Psychology: General, 2016): digitally reducing eyelid openness lowered perceived intelligence, independently of mouth curvature; the authors frame it as overgeneralising from cues of tiredness and mood. — [University of St Andrews news](https://news.st-andrews.ac.uk/archive/want-to-appear-more-intelligent-get-more-sleep/)
- Akram & Barclay (2013 pilot, 63 participants): upper-eyelid height and eyebrow elevation are the two factors that shape perceived tiredness. — [Northumbria research portal](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/what-characterizes-the-perception-of-a-tired-face-a-pilot-study/)
- Contempt is the one candidate universal expression that is **unilateral** (one-sided lip-corner raise/tightening, AU12/AU14 on one side); Ekman & Friesen (1986) found students in ten cultures matched it to "contempt", and Matsumoto (1992) replicated it. Russell (1991) criticised the forced-choice method. — [Matsumoto 1992 PDF](https://www.davidmatsumoto.com/content/1992%20More%20Evidence%20for%20the%20Universality%20of%20a%20Contempt%20Expression.pdf)

### Inferences
- Frank's gaze profile should be "confident introvert": relatively low mutual gaze while listening (about 40-55%, with long task-ward or side-ward fixations as in Andrist's introvert model), but a raised VDR, i.e. more direct gaze while *he* is speaking (about 60-75%). The VDR asymmetry is what turns introversion into aloof confidence and away from shyness.
- The "sidelong glance" is a compound move: the eyes turn 10-15 deg while the head-equivalent (here, nothing) stays still, the upper lids drop 15-25%, and one brow's outer end rises. Because Frank has no neck, eye-without-head is his natural state, which already reads as a sidelong look; the lids decide whether it reads skeptical (lowered) or alert (open).
- Talamas and the tiredness studies imply a floor on the resting lid level: heavy-lidded reads as tired and less intelligent, the opposite of Frank's intellect. Keep the resting upper lid only slightly lowered (about 70-80% of full open) and get "unimpressed" from brow shape and asymmetry instead.
- Contempt's unilaterality, and the general link between one-sidedness and social-evaluative expressions, support an asymmetry bias for skeptical, amused and irritated, while surprise and genuine fascination stay symmetric. The symmetric case then reads as more "genuine" by contrast (a hypothesis, see Gaps).
- Thinking aversions (look away up or sideways for 0.5-2 s before answering, then return) are the most cited "intelligent" gaze behaviour and suit "concentrating".

### Gaps
- Ekman, Hager & Friesen (1981, "The symmetry of emotional and deliberate facial actions", *Psychophysiology* 18) is usually cited as showing that deliberate actions are more asymmetric than spontaneous ones, but its findings were not retrieved here; treat the claim as unverified.
- No study found that measures perceived skepticism or "dryness" from brow or lid asymmetry on a robot or agent.
- Personality-gaze studies on humans (e.g. eye-tracking predictors of the Big Five) were not retrieved; the extraversion-gaze link here rests on Andrist's dyad data and Neff's literature summary.
- Bee et al. (2010) on expressing dominance through agent gaze was identified but not read.

## 5. Expressivity gain and exaggeration levels for robots, the "too much" risk, habituation and variation

### Takeaway
Gielniak & Thomaz give the only direct numbers on an exaggeration dial for a robot. On a normalised scale where 1.0 is the torque limit, viewers set "robot-like" at about 0.15, "human-like" at about 0.43, "cartoon-like" at about 0.89, and "liked best" at about 0.73. Exaggerated motion was more engaging, entertaining and memorable, but less realistic. For variation, the evidence (Ciardo et al. 2022; shipped systems such as Vector and Disney) says human-range timing variability and randomised variants read as alive, and identical repeats read as machine.

### Cited Findings
- Gielniak & Thomaz (HRI 2012) define exaggeration as greater contrast between primary motion (task/intent) and secondary motion (physics-based response). Experiment 1: 54 participants watched a 13-motion story told by a robot under four conditions (all exaggerated AE, 7 exaggerated then 6 unexaggerated EU, the reverse UE, all unexaggerated AU). Experiment 2: 68 participants, with faceLAB eye tracking. — [Gielniak & Thomaz 2012 PDF](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)
- Likert means (1-7) for AE vs AU: Entertaining 5.20 vs 3.67; Engaging 5.24 vs 3.53; Exaggerated 4.86 vs 3.13; Expressive 4.59 vs 3.39; Subtle 2.63 vs 5.35; Realistic 2.87 vs 4.21; Cartoon-like 3.34 vs 2.50; Human-like 4.12 vs 3.67; Natural 3.90 vs 3.70; Smooth 5.49 vs 5.26. Exaggerated motion was rated less realistic but more cartoon-like. — [Gielniak & Thomaz 2012, Table 1](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)
- Exaggeration dial set by participants (alpha_exp averaged over 13 motions, where 1.0 means any more would exceed at least one motor's torque limit and unexaggerated input motions sat at -0.2 to 0.0): Most Human-like 0.433, Most Cartoon-like 0.893, Most Robot-like 0.150, Most Visually Pleasing 0.771, Liked Best 0.733. 122 of 130 post-hoc pairings differed at p < 0.05, and the values were largely independent of the motion. — [Gielniak & Thomaz 2012, Table 2](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)
- Preference: across conditions, 62.2% picked a favourite story part tied to an exaggerated motion and 64.8% picked an exaggerated motion as their favourite (75% and 80% in the two mixed conditions where a real choice existed). Reasons given: "animated", "expressive", "emotional". Exaggeration also made observers' gaze more predictable and improved recall of story details. — [Gielniak & Thomaz 2012](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)
- Ciardo, De Tommaso & Wykowska (Science Robotics 2022, iCub, joint Simon task, nonverbal Turing test): pre-programmed robot reaction times within the human range but without a human-like distribution still let participants detect the human above chance; the authors conclude that the range of behavioural variability is a cue for attributing humanness. — [TechXplore summary](https://techxplore.com/news/2022-07-human-like-features-robot-behavior-response.amp); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/959924)
- A RO-MAN 2023 paper (21 participants) found that more action variability and persistence raised a social robot's likability and engagement. — [University of Florence record](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/1386316)
- Perlin (IEEE TVCG 1995, "Real time responsive animation with personality") drives time-varying puppet parameters with rhythmic and stochastic noise functions, avoiding dynamics simulation; the related patent describes each rotation axis as having a time-dependent term that can be a sine, cosine or noise function. — [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/468392); [NYU Improv project](https://mrl.cs.nyu.edu/projects/improv/); [US6115053](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6115053)
- Disney's animatronic built its default "Read" behaviour from several keyframed eye variants chosen at random each loop (`random_select(eyes_read_A, B, C)`), so the base loop does not visibly repeat. — [Pan et al. 2020, Algorithm 1](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/root.pdf)
- Disney's attention engine habituates: a person's curiosity score is multiplied by a habituation factor Theta in [0, 1] that falls at rate m_hab while the robot looks at them and recovers at rate m_rest when it does not, so the character does not fixate on one guest. The authors note animators can make a nervous character that reacts to the slightest stimulus, or a drowsy one that reacts only to strong stimuli, by tuning these weights. — [Pan et al. 2020, Eqns. 1-2 and §V-B](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/root.pdf)
- Repeated live interactions alone reduced a robot's perceived eeriness regardless of its form; likeability depended mainly on the robot's attitude. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2015, persistence of the uncanny valley](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00883/text)

### Inferences
- Gielniak's dial suggests Frank's routine expressions should sit near the "human-like" setting (about 0.4 of the available range above neutral), with the fascinated burst going up to the "liked best / visually pleasing" band (about 0.75) and touching the cartoon band (about 0.9) only rarely. Staying near 0.15 everywhere would read "robot-like". This is the quantitative basis for an expressivity gain that is low by default and spikes when earned.
- Note that Gielniak's dial is full-body joint-space exaggeration on a humanoid, not facial; the absolute numbers are a starting point for tuning, not a spec.
- Variation should be on timing as well as amplitude, and drawn from a distribution and not a fixed pattern (Ciardo). Practical recipe: 2-4 authored variants per tag, weighted random selection, per-play jitter, a no-immediate-repeat rule and a repetition penalty (as in Vector, section 6).
- Habituation should apply to Frank's own reactions too: the third "surprised" at the same stimulus within a minute should be smaller and slower (or turn into "unimpressed"), which is both lifelike and in character.

### Gaps
- No study found that quantifies the uncanny risk of over-exaggerating a *realistic silicone face* (Gielniak's robot was a mechanical humanoid). The uncanny literature on facial-motion intensity for realistic android faces (e.g. Geminoid studies) was not retrieved.
- No study found that isolates identical repeated animation against varied animation over weeks of interaction; the habituation evidence is indirect.
- Ciardo et al.'s full statistics were not read (press and abstract only).

## 6. How shipped characters store personality as parameters (Cozmo/Vector, Disney animatronics, Furhat, others)

### Takeaway
Anki's Vector is the best-documented shipped example: a five-dimension emotion state (Happy, Confident, Social, Stimulated, Trust), each with value ranges, event-driven "affectors", designer-drawn decay curves and a repetition penalty, plus slower "simple moods". Mood then selects among animation variants by weighted random choice. Disney's research animatronic exposes personality as attention weights and habituation rates plus a subsumption stack of behaviour layers. Furhat stores a "character" as roughly 100 face parameters plus a voice. No public trait-slider system was found for Furhat, Ameca or Pixar's procedural characters.

### Cited Findings
- Vector's emotion model has five dimensions: Stimulated (from sensory input: sound, wake word, faces/gaze, touch, motion), Social ("how eager [he] is to interact with users generally"), Confident (rises with success, falls with failure, e.g. missing the cube), Happy ("overall, things are going well") and Trust (added in v1.6; Vector originally had four and Cozmo reportedly had nine). The SDK ranges are Confident, Happy and Social -1 to 1, and Stimulated and Trust 0 to 1. The manual notes "when more dimensions are used, it is harder to get them right, and the less convincing the character is when they aren't." — [Randall Maas, Anki Vector Technical Reference Manual (2021), ch. 29 and SDK mood table](https://randym32.github.io/Vector-TRM.pdf)
- Mechanics: the behaviour engine posts named emotion events. Each maps to emotion affectors (dimension plus value, usually -1 to 1), an optional repetition penalty (a "time ratio" curve that shrinks the effect of repeated events), and decay graphs. The decay graphs use time nodes ("ratio of the original value that should be reached by" t seconds since the last significant change) or value-slope nodes (fixed decay per minute toward zero). A mood_config.json holds decay graphs, default repetition penalty, allowed value ranges per dimension, and an audio parameter map that sets audio parameters from the current emotion values. Slower "simple moods" (Default, Frustrated, HighStim, LowStim, MedStim) map to ranges of the emotion values and change "at a much slower rate". — [Vector TRM §121](https://randym32.github.io/Vector-TRM.pdf)
- Animation selection: an animation trigger names a group; the engine screens the group's animations for the current mood, then picks one by normalised weights (typically 1.0 each), so "playing the same trigger twice may not result in the exact same underlying animation playing twice". Group entries can also randomise head angle within a range. — [Vector TRM, animation-group section](https://randym32.github.io/Vector-TRM.pdf)
- Vector's procedural face separates the animated eye shape (per-eye upper and lower lid Y position, angle and bend; corner radii; face centre, scale and tilt) from gaze, which the procedural face manager owns: it "controls the blink rate, the focus of the eyes and how much the eyes dart around", and handles eye contact. In other words, gaze habits and blink are engine parameters, not baked into animations. The upper lid arc creates "sleepy, frustrated/angry" looks; the lower lid/cheek arc creates "happy". — [Vector TRM §106](https://randym32.github.io/Vector-TRM.pdf)
- Stimulation is the master excitability knob: "When stimulation is low, the robot is chill… studiously observing but not acting out"; noise, eye contact or his name spike it, and at high stimulation he leaves the charger to socialise (Anki's Brad Neuman, quoted). The audio engine also scales with it: "the more stimulated Vector is, the more animated his sounds will be". — [Vector TRM §121.1 and audio chapter](https://randym32.github.io/Vector-TRM.pdf)
- Cozmo's emotion engine responds to events (e.g. stacking cubes raises confidence; detecting a cliff lowers "braveness"); Anki described personality as layered on top of movement, exploration and emotional stimuli. Anki wrote "a huge amount of software inside of Maya" so animators could animate the robot directly. — [Wikipedia: Cozmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cozmo); [Design News](https://www.designnews.com/testing-measurement/from-cozmo-to-vector-how-anki-designs-robots-with-emotional-intelligence)
- Cozmo's design team dropped pupils from the eyes because they made him feel too human, and resized the eyes instead to show where he was looking. — [Core77](https://www.core77.com/posts/54328/Whats-the-One-Design-Element-That-Can-Make-a-Robot-Lovable)
- Anki hired animators from Pixar and DreamWorks and built a feature-film-style pipeline for the robot. — [Fortune 2016](https://www.fortune.com/2016/06/27/anki-pixar-dreamworks-robot-games); [Wikipedia: Cozmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cozmo)
- Disney Research animatronic: a curiosity score per person, Phi = Theta(t) * w_distance * f(d) * [w_raised_hand(...) + w_hand(...) + w_nose(...) + w_distance f(d)] with f(d) = e^(-a d). Its weights are explicitly the personality knobs: "This design enables character creators to shape the interactive personality of the character through modification of weights". State machine: Read (default), Glance (Phi above a glance threshold; head tilt grows with Phi; timeout a function of Phi), Engage (Phi above engage threshold; minimum timeout), Acknowledge (familiar guests). Subsumption layers: L0 base, L1 Alive (breathing, blinking, saccades), L2 Read, L3 Glance, L4 Engage, L5 Acknowledge. Changing saccade, blink or breathing speed means changing one layer and not regenerating every trajectory. The authors propose future learned dimension reduction to generate "attention profiles for a variety of characters". — [Pan et al. 2020 PDF](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/root.pdf)
- Furhat: the standard 3D face model has "close to one hundred parameters" for face shape and expressions; face, texture and voice can be saved as a "character" and recalled; built-in expressions can be edited "to suit your ideal robot persona", and users can record new gestures with iPhone ARKit. — [Furhat Robotics product page](https://furhatrobotics.com/furhat)

### Inferences
- Vector's architecture is a close template for Frank's engine: fast emotion dimensions (driven by LLM tags and events) with designer-drawn decay curves, a slow mood layer that biases baselines, a repetition penalty, and mood-filtered weighted selection among variants. The key design lesson is Maas's note: few dimensions, well tuned, beat many dimensions.
- Vector's split, where animations own shapes and the engine owns gaze and blink, maps directly to Frank: tags select lid, brow and mouth poses, while a separate always-on layer owns saccades, blink timing, gaze target choice and habituation.
- Disney's "the weights are the personality" approach (attention weights, habituation and restoration rates, thresholds and timeouts per state) is the right form for Frank's gaze-habit parameters.

### Gaps
- No published personality-parameter schema was found for Engineered Arts' Ameca (Tritium) or for Furhat beyond face parameters and saved characters.
- No published account of how Pixar's procedural or crowd characters, or game AI "personality profiles", are parameterised was retrieved (no GDC talk on Cozmo or Vector's procedural animation was found).
- The exact contents of Vector's shipped decay curves and affector values (the JSON files) were not read; only their schema is documented in the manual.

## 7. Proposed personality parameter set for Frank (INFERENCE: my synthesis, not a sourced result)

### Takeaway
Frank's personality can be one profile object that the engine applies to every LLM tag. It has (a) a resting baseline, (b) Effort-style dynamics (onset, acceleration, overshoot, hold, release), (c) an expressivity gain with a channel budget and a rolling "restraint" allowance, (d) asymmetry rules, (e) gaze and blink habits, (f) variation and habituation, and (g) a fascination override that flips the dynamics from Direct/Sustained/Bound to Indirect/Free/Sudden for short, earned bursts. Starting values below are anchored to the cited numbers where possible and are meant to be tuned on the physical head.

### Cited Findings
- Anchors used: PERFORM trait-to-Effort mapping and parameter list ([PERFORM](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/PERFORM_TOG.pdf)); Neff's introvert settings (amplitude about 0.5-0.8x, durations unchanged vs 80% for the extravert, low rate, longer latency) ([Neff et al. 2010](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~neff/papers/extraversion_final_PRE_PRINT.pdf)); Gielniak's exaggeration bands of 0.15 / 0.43 / 0.73 / 0.89 ([Gielniak & Thomaz 2012](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)); gaze fixation distributions ([Andrist et al. 2015](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2015/CHI15-Andrist.pdf)); 50% gaze friendliest ([Koda et al. 2017](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2017/62294/62294.pdf)); VDR ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Dominance_Ratio)); Disney's saccade timing of 0.1-0.5 s, eyes-lead-head and habituation equation ([Pan et al. 2020](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/root.pdf)); Vector's decay graphs, repetition penalty and weighted variant selection ([Vector TRM](https://randym32.github.io/Vector-TRM.pdf)).
- Blink anchors: Takashima et al. (Graphics Interface 2008) showed avatars blinking at 9, 12, 18, 24 and 36 blinks/min for 20 s each; 18/min gave the friendliest impression, 36/min read as inactive (a follow-up found higher rates read as nervous), and 9/min read as intelligent, with stronger effects for human-like avatars. — [Graphics Interface 2008](https://graphicsinterface.org/?p=4916); [DBLP record](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/graphicsinterface/TakashimaOYIKK08.html)
- Weibel et al. (J. Media Psychology 2010): avatars with large pupils and slow blink frequency were rated more sociable and attractive, below conscious awareness. — [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1027%2F1864-1105%2FA000005)
- Human baseline (Bentivoglio et al., Movement Disorders 1997, 150 subjects): blink rate is highest in conversation, lower at rest and lowest when reading (pattern conversation > rest > reading in 67.3% of subjects); commonly quoted means are about 17/min at rest, 26/min in conversation and 4.5/min reading, but the paper's percentage changes do not reconcile neatly with those means. — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/mds.870120629); [Scilit record](https://scilit.net/publications/4970d8a142cd82bf5d8297f233fde1ca)

### Inferences
All values are proposals. Amplitudes are fractions of each channel's calibrated range from neutral; times are per move.

**A. Resting baseline (Frank's "default face", what every tag is an offset from)**
- `rest.upper_lid`: 0.75 open (slightly heavy; do not go below about 0.65 at rest, or it reads tired and less intelligent per Talamas).
- `rest.lower_lid`: 0.1 raised (a faint narrowing that reads evaluative).
- `rest.brow`: inner ends neutral; one outer end (the `asym_side`, see D) raised about 0.10 so the skeptical habit is "always slightly on".
- `rest.mouth_corners`: -0.05 (a hair down); the `asym_side` corner tight at +0.03 (the latent smirk).
- `rest.jaw`: closed, lips together.
- `mood.baseline`: "mildly unimpressed" = low arousal, slightly negative evaluation, low social eagerness (in Vector terms: Stimulated 0.2, Social -0.2, Happy 0.0, Confident +0.5).

**B. Dynamics (Effort profile). Default is Direct / Sustained / Bound, with Strong accents**
- `onset_ms` (attack): brows 450-700, lids 300-500, mouth corners 400-600. Eyes always saccade fast (servo-limited), because slow eyes read sleepy and not restrained.
- `ease_exponent` (PERFORM's time exponent): 1.3-1.6, so moves ease out and settle gently.
- `overshoot`: 0.0-0.03 by default (Bound); 0.10-0.15 in fascination (Free).
- `anticipation`: a small counter-move of 0.03-0.05 for 80-150 ms before brow raises and surprise (Takayama's forethought); off for micro-moves.
- `accent_snap` (Strong/Sudden accent): a single fast channel move (e.g. a brow drop of 120-200 ms) allowed on irritated and skeptical beats while everything else stays slow; this is the arrogance seasoning (PERFORM: Sudden reads as disagreeable).
- `hold_s`: 1.5-4.0 s holds with a "moving hold" noise of 0.01-0.02 amplitude at 0.2-0.5 Hz (Perlin-style), never a dead freeze.
- `release`: Vector-style decay curve back to baseline, half-life 3-6 s for tags and 1-5 min for mood. Release is slower than attack (expressions "melt").
- `reaction_latency_ms`: 300-800 ms "considering" beat before reacting to user input (introvert latency, Neff); 0-150 ms in fascination or true surprise.

**C. Expressivity gain and restraint budget**
- `gain.default`: 0.45 (about Gielniak's "human-like" 0.43).
- `gain.fascinated`: 0.8 (about "liked best/visually pleasing" 0.73-0.77); cap 0.9.
- `gain.per_tag` multipliers, for example: neutral 0, skeptical 0.6, concentrating 0.5, amused 0.5 (a smirk, not a smile), irritated 0.6, confused 0.6, surprised 0.8, vulnerable 0.4, curious 0.6, excited 0.9 (only meaningful when the fascination gate is open).
- `channel_budget`: at most 2 feature groups (out of brows, lids, mouth, jaw-as-expression) changing at once by default, 3-4 in fascination. A stagger of 80-200 ms between groups when more than one moves ("the face shouldn't do everything at once").
- `restraint_allowance`: a rolling expressivity budget, e.g. the integral of |gain| over the last 60 s capped at a set level, refilling slowly. A big moment spends it, so the next few beats are forced small. This makes big moments rare and therefore readable.
- `laugh_threshold`: a full laugh (jaw plus both corners) only if amused intensity is above 0.9 and no laugh happened in the last 10 min; otherwise amused plays as a one-corner smirk, a lower-lid squeeze and an exhale-style jaw drop of 0.05.

**D. Asymmetry**
- `asym_side`: L or R (pick one and keep it for life, so it reads as his habit).
- `asym_magnitude`: 0.2-0.4 differential between sides for skeptical, amused, irritated and concentrating; 0.0-0.1 for surprised, vulnerable and fascinated (symmetry reads as genuine and drops the guard).
- `brow_inner_outer_split`: for skeptical, the `asym_side` outer end up 0.3-0.5 while the other side's inner end draws down 0.1-0.2 (the classic one-brow skeptic); for concentrating, both inners down 0.2 with outers neutral.
- `side_lead_ms`: the `asym_side` leads by 40-120 ms on onsets, so even symmetric targets get a natural offset.

**E. Gaze and blink habits**
- `gaze.mutual_listening`: 0.45 (introvert-leaning; near Fukayama's friendliest 50%, below the 100% that reads unfriendly).
- `gaze.mutual_speaking`: 0.65 (VDR of about 1.4, which reads as quiet dominance).
- `gaze.fixation_s`: draw from normal distributions (Andrist style): face fixation mean 1.6 s (SD 0.4) when he is listening and 2.5 s (SD 0.8) when he is speaking; away-fixations mean 2-4 s with wide SD.
- `gaze.thinking_aversion`: before answering a question, look up or to the `asym_side` for 0.6-2.0 s, then return on the first stressed word.
- `gaze.saccade_interval_s`: random 0.1-0.5 s between micro-saccades among the viewer's eyes and nose during mutual gaze (Disney), with amplitude 1-3 deg; larger shifts capped at about 12 deg (SmartBody/Eyes Alive) except deliberate looks.
- `gaze.space`: Direct by default (few targets, long dwell); Indirect in fascination (more targets, faster scanning: PERFORM's openness signature).
- `gaze.sidelong`: the skeptical gaze move is eyes 10-15 deg off the viewer, upper lids -0.15 to -0.25 and the `asym_side` brow outer end +0.3.
- `lids.track_eyes`: upper lids follow eye pitch (down-gaze lowers lids); the eyes lead and the lids follow by 30-60 ms (overlapping action).
- `blink.rate_per_min`: 10-12 while idle and listening (Takashima's 9/min read as intelligent; avoid 24-36, which read as nervous or inactive); 14-18 while speaking; blinks fire preferentially on large gaze shifts and phrase ends.
- `blink.duration_ms`: 150-250 normal; an occasional slow "unimpressed blink" of 400-600 ms closure, at most about once per minute, as a deadpan punctuation beat.

**F. Variation and habituation**
- `variants_per_tag`: 2-4, chosen by weighted random selection filtered by mood (Vector); no immediate repeat of the same variant.
- `jitter`: amplitude ±10-15%, timing ±15-20%, drawn from a distribution (Ciardo: human-range variability reads as human).
- `repetition_penalty`: the same tag within 30 s plays at 0.6x gain, a third time at 0.4x or converts to "unimpressed" (Vector's repetition-penalty curve).
- `stimulus_habituation`: per-stimulus interest Theta with m_hab of about -0.1/s while attended and m_rest of about +0.02/s while not (Disney Eqn. 2); low Theta damps surprised and curious.

**G. Fascination override (the "restraint drops" state)**
- `fascination.trigger`: the LLM tags curious or excited at high intensity (or a dedicated flag) and the restraint allowance is available.
- `fascination.effort`: switch to Indirect + Free + mildly Sudden: onsets 150-300 ms, overshoot 0.10-0.15, gain 0.8, channel budget 4, symmetric (asym 0.0-0.1), eyes widen (upper lid 0.95-1.0), mutual gaze up to 0.7 with faster scanning.
- `fascination.duration`: 3-10 s, then decay over 4-8 s back to baseline (not a snap), spending the restraint allowance so the following minute is extra dry. This contrast is what makes the moment read as earned.

**H. Speech-time overlay**
- `speech.jaw_gain`: 0.6-0.8 of a lip-sync's full range (a dry, contained delivery); the lips and corners carry the attitude, the jaw only articulates.
- `speech.brow_accents`: at most one brow accent per phrase, on the stressed word, amplitude 0.15-0.3, `asym_side` biased.
- `speech.gaze`: follows the VDR rule above, with a brief look-away at phrase starts on longer turns.

### Gaps
- All numbers in this section are untested proposals. They need tuning on the physical head with its real servo speeds, silicone damping and viewing distance; Disney notes that believability depends on time and distance, so lower-fidelity behaviour can pass at range.
- No published study validates facial (as opposed to body) Effort parameters, asymmetry magnitudes or a "restraint budget"; these are craft-derived.
- The blink-rate anchors come from screen avatars (Takashima) and may shift for a physical silicone face.
