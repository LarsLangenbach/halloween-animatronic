# Animation craft and runtime animation architecture for believable robot and animatronic faces

Scope: animation principles, layering and blending, gaze, blinking, idle motion, speech-face coordination and expression timing, read for Frank's face performance engine (laptop Python at 25-50 Hz, Pico, hobby servos: eyes L/R and U/D, 4 lids, 4-servo brow, 2-servo jaw, 4 lip servos, 4 corner servos; no neck).

Full texts read for these notes: Ribeiro & Paiva 2012, Takayama/Dooley/Ju 2011, Andrist et al. 2014, Trutoiu et al. 2011, Pan et al. (Disney) 2020. The rest comes from abstracts and secondary summaries, and is flagged where that matters.

## 1. Which animation principles matter most for a robot face (van Breemen, Takayama/Dooley/Ju, Ribeiro & Paiva, Anki, Hoffman & Ju)

### Takeaway
The face-relevant principles with direct evidence or explicit robot guidance are: slow in/slow out with soft blending between animations (van Breemen's "merging logic"); anticipation/forethought before an action and a reaction after it, which measurably raise appeal and perceived intelligence; secondary action (random blinks, breathing) as the always-on "alive" layer; timing as an emotional dial, since speed and acceleration read as arousal; exaggeration, which raises engagement once it is strong enough to notice; and asymmetry, which comes under "solid drawing". Squash and stretch mostly does not apply to rigid robots.

### Cited Findings
- van Breemen defined robot animation as "the process of computing how the robot should act such that it is believable and interactive" — [Nutty-based Robot Animation, Ribeiro & Paiva 2019 (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898)
- van Breemen's 2004 CHI workshop paper "Bringing robots to life: Applying principles of animation to robots" argued that user-interface robots have the same problem as early animation, a missing illusion of life — [Ribeiro & Paiva, "The illusion of robotic life", HRI 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf); [search record of the 2004 citation](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2101506210)
- van Breemen's IROS 2004 paper "Animation engine for believable interactive user-interface robots" (pp. 2873-2878, DOI 10.1109/IROS.2004.1389845) describes an engine that combines multiple interactive behaviours with believable pre-made animations. Its example is the iCat speaking while tracking the user's head, lip-syncing, blinking and showing facial expressions, all at once — [indexed abstract](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2101506210). A follow-up "Advanced Animation Engine for User-Interface Robots" appeared at IROS 2006 (pp. 1824-1830) — [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/iros/BreemenX06.html)
- iCat was "mainly controlled by 'playing' animations, predefined sequences of actuator values" — [later iCat work, AI & Society 2009 (search summary)](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00146-009-0198-1.pdf)
- Slow in/slow out is "one of the major ones to apply" for robots: "animations should softly blend one into another, or in and out of the character. Van Breemen called this Merging Logic when he applied it in Philips's iCat." Their example: "When a character smiles, its mouth doesn't suddenly turn to a smile, it slowly blends into it." — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)
- Ribeiro & Paiva's mapping of each principle to robots, from the same paper ([PDF](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)):
  - Squash and stretch is "hard to apply to robots, because robots are generally composed of rigid parts."
  - Anticipation helps users understand what the character is about to do.
  - Follow-through works "as an opposite of Anticipation". A character "shouldn't stop abruptly", and follow-through also marks "that an action has ended."
  - Arcs: when looking left and right, add some vertical movement instead of a pure horizontal sweep. This works for procedural motion as well as pre-designed motion.
  - Secondary action: "One simple case of applying this to robots can be adding random blinks to the eyes, and adding a soft, slow sinusoidal motion to the body to simulate breathing."
  - Timing: "the same movement, pre-animated or procedural, can have different meanings depending on the timing used." Saerbeck & Bartneck found that a robot's acceleration correlates with its perceived arousal.
  - Solid drawing: "The main concept to get from this principle is asymmetry. Faces rarely exhibit the same expression on the left and right sides, and almost no poses are symmetrical, unless one wants to convey the feeling of stiffness."
  - Staging: use multimodal cues (lights, sound) to make the intention clear.
  - Straight-ahead versus pose-to-pose maps to procedural (interactive) motion versus pre-designed animation that "can be synced and blended with other kinds of behaviours."
- In the EMYS study, applying the principles fixed the expressions that had failed. Disgust got more eye asymmetry (one eye closing more than the other), more horizontal panning, a head that returned "in a more resistive way" and eyes kept pressed longer. Anger got eyelids that "not only close, but tremble while closed". "Surprise was made more instant." Fear got a chin tremble and a negative "pleading" nod. All expressions were also refined for arcs, slow in/out and follow-through. Recognition in phase 2 (61 participants, 4-s clips) had over 90% of viewers getting at least two of three intensities right for anger, fear and sadness, over 60% for surprise and joy, and under 50% for disgust — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)
- EMYS encoded each expressive feature (jaw, lower lids, lip corners and so on) on a −5…0…+5 scale with five intensities per emotion. For example, happiness 1 = lower lids −1, jaw 0, lip corners +1, and happiness 5 = lower lids −5, jaw +5, lip corners +5. A smile "is expressed especially with the corners of the mouth, and lower eyelids" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)
- Puppetry (Jim Henson) is a closer model than cartoons because puppets "are subject to the laws of physics and our real world". Happiness reads as bouncing, fear as a trembling mouth and withdrawal, anger as a head tilted toward the target with the mouth closed — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)
- Takayama, Dooley (Pixar) & Ju, HRI 2011, used PR2 animations in a video study with a 2 (forethought vs. none) × 2 (reaction vs. none) × 2 (success vs. failure) design ([PDF](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf)):
  - Showing forethought (anticipation) made viewers more sure of their interpretation (M = 5.69 vs 5.20 on a 1-7 scale, p < .0001), but did not improve keyword-match accuracy (p = .37, n.s.).
  - Forethought raised appeal (4.83 vs 4.27, p < .001) and approachability (5.05 vs 4.54, p < .001).
  - A goal-oriented reaction to the outcome (happy on success, disappointed on failure) made the robot seem smarter (competence/intelligence 4.72 vs 3.86, p < .001) whether it succeeded or failed.
  - Expressive layers: "engagement" was a very small forward or backward translation, and "confidence" a small torso rise (positive) or drop (negative). "Together with timing, this element was critical."
  - They quote Lasseter: "All the movements and actions of a character are the result of its thought process."
- Gielniak & Thomaz (HRI 2012) defined exaggeration as more contrast between primary (intent) motion and secondary (physics-response) motion. It "is perceptibly different from the input motion" only when strong enough, and its levels map onto expectations of robot-like, human-like and cartoon-like motion. Exaggerated motion increased engagement and perceived entertainment value, made partners' gaze direction predictable and improved retention of interaction details — [Gielniak & Thomaz 2012](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)
- Hoffman & Ju (JHRI 2014, 3(1):89-122) argue for designing robots "with movement in mind". Appearance sets context and expectations, "but movement is critical for conveying more dynamic information", and "far less attention has been paid" to accurate expressive motion — [Hoffman & Ju 2014](https://doi.org/10.5898/JHRI.3.1.Hoffman); later work cites it for the idea that movement conveys "purpose, intent, state, character, attention, and capabilities" — [ELEGNT 2025](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2501.12493)
- Anki (Cozmo): the GDC 2017 talk "Cozmo: Animation Pipeline for a Physical Robot" (Jerjomina & Jameson) covered moving Maya animation data onto a robot with physical constraints, streaming audio and animation to the robot, and when programmatic corrections were needed — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024221/Cozmo-Animation-Pipeline-for-a). Anki's "emotion engine" is described as "a collection of algorithms that affect the way the robot mimics feelings", built around Ekman's core emotions — [etcentric](https://www.etcentric.org/?p=105621); [Wikipedia: Cozmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cozmo)
- Disney's IROS 2020 animatronic paper puts the principles into practice. "Secondary action" is the always-on L1 layer (breathing, blinks, saccades). "Overlapping action" is implemented as per-joint motion bandwidth: lids 10 Hz, head ≤5 Hz, with the eyes always leading the head — [Pan et al. 2020 (Disney Research), full text](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Frank, rank the principles by impact: (1) slow in/out and merging, with every channel eased and every new target blended from the current state, never snapped; (2) secondary action always on (blinks, saccades, micro-noise, breathing-like brow and lip drift); (3) timing per feature, so eyes, lids, brows and mouth get different time constants, giving the "distinct timing" the character bible asks for; (4) anticipation before big changes (a small opposite-direction dip or a glance before the brow rises or the mouth opens on a speaking turn) and reaction/follow-through after them (overshoot and settle); (5) asymmetry, with a per-side offset or delay on brows and corners; (6) exaggeration, scaled by an "intensity" or "theatricality" gain on top of the target poses.
- Takayama's result says anticipation makes the robot more appealing even when it does not make it more legible. So "pre-speech" anticipation (breath-in jaw drop, brow lift, glance away and back) is worth adding before Speaking starts, regardless of whether it helps comprehension.
- A goal-oriented reaction raised perceived intelligence, so every orchestrator event (the person answered, an interruption, a farewell) should trigger a short visible reaction on top of the state's base behaviour.
- EMYS's −5…+5 per-feature intensity grid suits Frank's emotion-to-pose mapping. Store each emotion as a feature vector at intensity 5 and scale it (with per-feature curves) for lower intensities.

### Gaps
- van Breemen's IROS 2004/2006 full text (the actual merging logic and transition-filter maths) could not be retrieved, because the IEEE copy is paywalled and the patent page returned 403. Ribeiro & Paiva's description is second-hand.
- The Anki GDC talk content is members-only. I found no public primary source on Cozmo/Vector procedural face layering.
- The Hoffman & Ju design recommendations were not read in full; only the abstract and introduction were.
- Takayama et al. used a PR2 (no face), so the effect sizes are for body-level anticipation and reaction, not facial expressions.

## 2. Runtime architecture: layers, additive blending, state machines, per-joint masks, procedural noise and look-at controllers

### Takeaway
Two published animatronic and robot systems give a complete, codeable recipe. Disney's subsumption stack (L0 zero pose → L1 alive → L2 base show → L3 glance → L4 engage → L5 acknowledge overlay) uses composable "show objects" (group, sequence, loop, random-select, add, scale, subtract). Andrist et al. blend several simultaneous gaze goals (face tracking, aversion, Perlin idle) through a predictive filter, so motions are interruptible instead of serial. Game engines add per-bone layered blending (masks) and additive layers on top of a base pose.

### Cited Findings
- Disney Research / Walt Disney Imagineering (Pan et al., IROS 2020) worked on a 19-DOF Audio-Animatronics bust (neck, eyes, lids, brows). The architecture has an attention engine, a behaviour selection engine (a hand-crafted state machine: Read, Glance, Engage, Acknowledge) and a behaviour library — [Pan et al. 2020 full text](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/disney-research-robotic-gaze)
- Disney's "show objects": "Show objects can be grouped (made to run simultaneously), sequenced (run in order one after another), looped (repeated for a duration), and randomly selected (pick one show object randomly from a set)... Additionally, show objects can be added together, multiplied by scalars, or subtracted from each other." Their reading show, as pseudocode: head down −0.33 rad over 1.0 s, eyes down −0.40 rad over 1.0 s, eyes left 0.20 rad, lids to 70% over 1.0 s, grouped into a pose. The head then follows keyframes [0.20 rad @0 s, −0.20 rad @3.8 s, 0.20 rad @4.6 s], and the eye reading pattern is a random pick from A/B/C, looped — [Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)
- Disney's layers ("higher levels are able to 'subsume' lower levels, i.e., they are able to integrate, modify, suppress or even completely override lower levels"), from [Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf):
  - L0 Zero Show: default motor positions, for example the jaw held closed rather than hanging open.
  - L1 Alive Show: breathing, eye blinking and saccades, the "secondary action" that does "not interfere with higher level behaviors".
  - L2 Read Show: the primary, always-running fallback.
  - L3 Glance: a higher curiosity score turns the head further toward the stimulus.
  - L4 Engage: subsumes read and glance. "Normal eye saccading from the L1 alive show is interrupted to saccade based on a person's eye and nose locations", and the brows furrow.
  - L5 Acknowledge: a random nod from a mocap-derived set, "as an overlay on top of the engage show".
- Disney on why layering pays off: "Changing something like how fast the robot saccades, blinks or breathes only involves changing a single behavioral layer, rather than requiring the regeneration of an entire set of motor trajectories." — [Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)
- Disney's attention engine works like this ([Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)):
  - Each person's curiosity score is a habituation factor times a weighted sum of normalised hand and nose velocities, hands raised above the shoulder, and a distance term f(d) = e^(−a·d_nose).
  - Habituation follows h(t+Δt) = h(t) + (B·m_hab + (1−B)·m_rest)·Δt, clipped to [0, 1]. B = 1 while the robot is looking at that person, m_hab is a negative decay rate and m_rest a positive restoration rate.
  - Glance fires above one threshold and Engage above a higher one. States time out as a function of the score.
  - The weights are a personality knob: "character creators [can] shape the interactive personality of the character through modification of weights."
- Andrist, Tan, Gleicher & Mutlu (HRI 2014) combine three simultaneous head goals (gaze aversions, face tracking and idle motion) through a Kalman filter: "Without this filter, head motions would have to be serially generated and completed... The Kalman filter solves this problem by blending estimated trajectories over time, resulting in smooth, interruptible motions." Filter gains were "chosen empirically to provide an appropriate balance between smoothness and reactivity." Face tracking was polled every 200 ms — [Andrist et al. 2014](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2014/HRI14-Andrist.pdf)
- Andrist idle motion: "a small amount of structured noise, generated by a Perlin noise function, [added] to the results of the face tracking algorithm. A Perlin noise function generates band-limited, pseudorandom signals that are useful for emulating biological forms and motions." It was added because otherwise, when the user sits still, "the robot's head becomes motionless and loses its sense of liveliness." Gaze-shift velocity profiles were ease-in/ease-out Bézier curves that respect both the upper and the effective lower motor speed limits — [Andrist et al. 2014](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2014/HRI14-Andrist.pdf)
- Ken Perlin's "Layered compositing of facial expression" (SIGGRAPH 97 Visual Proceedings, pp. 226-227) is a stratified real-time approach where "animators layer degrees of freedom recursively to relate lower-level facial movements to higher-level moods and intentions" — [SIGGRAPH history](https://history.siggraph.org/learning/layered-compositing-of-facial-expression-by-perlin). His Responsive Face demo has a "noise" toggle that switches off "the little controlled random movements that bring the character to life" — [Responsive Face](https://artport.whitney.org/gatepages/artists/perlin/facedemo/text.html). He describes expressions being "mixed to simulate shifting moods and attitudes" "without using repetitive prebuilt animations" — [Perlin bio](https://cs.nyu.edu/~perlin/doc/bio-page.html)
- Unreal Engine's layered animation offers Blend Spaces, "additive blending by weighted alpha" and direct overrides. A montage plays in a named slot, and "Layered Blend Per Bone" applies it from a chosen bone down (for example spine_01, with a blend depth spreading the blend over that many child bones) — [Epic docs: Using Layered Animations](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/using-layered-animations-in-unreal-engine). Several layered-blend nodes can be chained, and montages in the same slot group conflict, so the fix is separate slot groups per body region — [Unreal forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/layered-blend-per-bone-and-multiple-slots/319781)
- SAIBA is cited as a popular behaviour-planning framework, used mainly for humanoid characters — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)
- PAD (pleasure-arousal-dominance) gives continuous emotion and seamless transitions, but "when transitioning from emotion A to emotion B, the transition may go through an emotion C, which can be invalid." — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)

### Inferences
- A layer stack for Frank, run at a fixed 50 Hz tick in Python (each layer outputs per-channel targets plus a weight or mask):
  - L0 neutral pose (jaw closed, lips relaxed).
  - L1 alive: blink scheduler, saccade/microsaccade scheduler, Perlin drift on brows, corners and lids at ~0.1-0.5 Hz with ±1-3% of range, and a slow "breath" sinusoid on the jaw and lips.
  - L2 emotion base pose from the synthesiser (continuous, slow).
  - L3 controller-state behaviour (Listening, Thinking gaze aversion, Idle look-around, Greeting).
  - L4 speech (jaw, lips and corners from audio, brow beats).
  - L5 one-shot reflexes and overlays (startle on Interrupted, nod-equivalent brow flash, tag-triggered gesture).
- Composition rules per channel, so the layers don't fight:
  - Eyes: gaze target from the highest active layer that owns gaze, plus L1 saccade offsets. Use override, not additive.
  - Upper lids: openness = min(emotion_openness, gaze-follow lid limit) × (1 − blink_closure). A blink multiplies openness and always wins.
  - Brows: emotion base + additive speech-beat offset + additive noise, clamped to the hard stops.
  - Jaw: speech overrides the emotion jaw while talking, with the emotion jaw as the floor (max/blend).
  - Corners and lips: emotion base + additive speech shaping, with speech weight cross-faded in and out over 100-200 ms.
- Andrist's Kalman blend can be replaced by something simpler that does the same job at 50 Hz: a per-channel critically damped second-order filter (spring-damper) whose target is the layer-composed value. It gives slow in/out, interruptibility and continuous velocity, with one natural-frequency parameter per channel (high for eyes and lids, low for brows and corners). That frequency is the "motion bandwidth" knob from Disney's paper.
- Disney's "show object" algebra (group/sequence/loop/random/add/scale) maps cleanly onto Python generator objects yielding per-channel targets per tick. It lets brow poses per tag become reusable clips that the orchestrator can scale (exaggeration), add (blend an emotion with a tag gesture) and randomly vary (no identical loops).
- The PAD caveat (A→B passing through an invalid C) argues for blending pose vectors directly between the current and target emotion poses rather than interpolating in PAD space and re-mapping every tick. Alternatively, keep PAD for the synthesiser and interpolate in pose space in the animator.

### Gaps
- I found no GDC talk with published numbers on look-at controllers (for example per-joint weight distributions or angular velocity limits). The Unreal docs give the concepts, not parameters.
- I did not find published numbers for "breathing layer" amplitude or frequency on animatronic faces. Disney names breathing but gives no rate.
- I did not read Perlin's full sketch, so the actual layering maths is unknown.

## 3. Eye and gaze models: saccades, fixations, microsaccades, eye-head coordination, gaze aversion, lids following gaze, Disney realistic gaze

### Takeaway
Saccade duration follows the main sequence (≈21 ms + 2.2 ms/deg, so a 10° saccade is ~43 ms, about two frames at 50 Hz). Saccade intervals while engaging a face can be random in 0.1-0.5 s, jumping between the person's two eyes and nose (Disney). Microsaccades run at about 1-2 Hz and under 1°. For conversation, Andrist's data give ready-made Gaussian parameters for cognitive (thinking), intimacy and floor-management aversions, and getting the timing wrong cancels the benefit. Humans move the eyes alone for shifts under ~20°, which suits a neckless Frank. The upper lid follows vertical gaze.

### Cited Findings
- Main sequence, linear form: duration = 2.2 × amplitude(°) + 21 ms (Carpenter 1988) — [Eye-gaze metrics paper (arXiv 1906.07183)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.07183). Reported parameter ranges for eye animation: intercept 20-30 ms, slope 2-2.7 ms/deg — [Duchowski et al., JEMR](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/download/3603/Duchowski_JEMR_10-3-4/13042). The relation is roughly linear for saccades up to ~80° — [Main sequence review (search summary)](https://iris.unige.it/retrieve/e268c4ce-65fa-a6b7-e053-3a05fe0adea1/Main_Sequence_BRM%20%28revised%29.pdf)
- Peak velocity saturates with amplitude, typically modelled with an asymptote of ~500°/s — [Main sequence review (search summary; not checked against full text)](https://iris.unige.it/retrieve/e268c4ce-65fa-a6b7-e053-3a05fe0adea1/Main_Sequence_BRM%20%28revised%29.pdf)
- Lee, Badler & Badler, "Eyes Alive" (SIGGRAPH 2002, ACM TOG 21(3):637-644), built a statistical saccade model separately for talking and for listening (magnitude, direction, duration, velocity, inter-saccadic interval) — [SIGGRAPH history](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=118454). The SmartBody toolkit implements "talk", "listen" and "think" saccade modes from it — [SmartBody docs (search snippet; page now 404)](https://smartbody.ict.usc.edu/HTML/documentation/SB/Eye-Saccade_8552851.html)
- Microsaccades are "fixational saccades" at a typical rate of 1-2 Hz, about 0.5 per second during free viewing. Amplitude criteria are usually ≤1-2°, measured means are about 0.4-0.5°, and durations are ~10-20 ms — [Frontiers in Neurology 2018 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2018.00144/full); [PMC7166123 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7166123/table/T1)
- Eye-head coordination: for horizontal gaze shifts under 20° with the eyes starting centred, the head contributes nothing. For 25-90° shifts the head's share grows linearly, and eye amplitude saturates at ~35° (rhesus monkeys, Freedman & Sparks 1997) — [abstract](https://scilit.com/publications/34e4450df8444ec1764e7e926bbc69a6). The head's share is larger when the eyes start deviated toward the target, and for auditory targets the head often moves first — [PMC3191847](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3191847)
- In Disney's animatronic, "with any turn-to-glance or turn-to-engage movement, the head turning is always led with the eyes, i.e., the eyes reach the gaze position first at a high rate of speed, followed by the head at a lower rate of speed" — [Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)
- Disney saccades: "Our Audio-Animatronics figure is able to saccade with a frequency of 20 Hz. For our mutual gaze actions (glance and engage), the eyes saccade randomly between three points forming a triangle on a person-of-interest: the eyes and nose. Timing of saccadic movements is random with a range of 0.1 to 0.5 s." — [Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)
- Disney: "saccades appear to significantly improve character realism". The bust's eyes are mechanically linked with parallel sightlines, so with a steady stare "the robot appears to look through you instead of at you". Saccading across the eyes and nose "provide[s] the illusion of vergence", because people look at one of the robot's eyes and then the other — [Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)
- Andrist et al. 2014 conversational gaze aversion, coded from 24 dyadic conversations (κ = .747). Every parameter is a Gaussian (M, SD); the values below are all from [Andrist et al. 2014](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2014/HRI14-Andrist.pdf):
  - Cognitive aversion (deep thought, for example at the start of an answer): length 3.54 s (1.26), starting 1.32 s (0.47) before the cognitive event and ending 2.23 s (0.63) after it.
  - Intimacy-modulating aversion while speaking: length 1.96 s (0.32), with 4.75 s (1.39) between aversions.
  - Intimacy-modulating aversion while listening: length 1.14 s (0.27), with 7.21 s (1.88) between aversions.
  - Floor management: length 2.30 s (1.10), starting 1.03 s (0.39) before the next utterance starts and ending 1.27 s (0.51) after.
  - At the end of a floor-passing utterance, mutual gaze is engaged 2.41 s (0.56) before the end, and no more aversions are generated.
  - Direction: cognitive aversions are more likely upward, intimacy and floor-management aversions more likely sideways.
- Andrist's robot adaptation (NAO, head only): aversion magnitudes of ~20° up, 28° horizontal and 22° down. Intimacy aversions are scaled by 0.4 because in humans they are frequent and subtle, often "eyes-only" — [Andrist et al. 2014](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2014/HRI14-Andrist.pdf)
- Andrist results (30 participants):
  - Robots using aversions were seen as more thoughtful.
  - Well-timed aversions held the floor longer: mean 608 ms before the participant interrupted the robot's planned 2-4 s pause, against 329 ms with static gaze and 327 ms with reversed ("bad") timing.
  - Human turn gaps are mostly under 400 ms, about a third under 200 ms.
  - Disclosure (word count) did not differ.
  — [Andrist et al. 2014](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2014/HRI14-Andrist.pdf); [Microsoft Research abstract](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=557574)
- Lid saccades: the upper lid moves with vertical eye saccades with similar metrics ("lid saccades", Becker & Fuchs 1988), acting as a follower of eye position — [review (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/18513071.pdf). In 19 adults making 20° up/down eye movements, upper-lid saccades were ~9 mm in amplitude both ways. Peak lid velocity was higher downward (young 71.8 mm/s down vs 59.1 mm/s up), and amplitude and velocity were linearly correlated (r = 0.71-0.75) — [Malbouisson et al. 2005, Braz J Med Biol Res](https://www.scielo.br/j/bjmbr/a/rLYRr9V5zp6t3MkyRVfr84N/?lang=en). Animal work suggests a shared vertical position signal keeps the lid just above the pupil during fixation (Chen & May) — [search summary](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/cne.21203)
- Ruhland et al. 2015 (Computer Graphics Forum 34(6):299-326) is the standard review of eyeball, eyelid and head movement models for virtual agents and robots. It ends with "a summary of guidelines for animating the eye and head from the perspective of a character animator" — [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org:443/xmlui/handle/10.1111/cgf12603)

### Inferences
- At 50 Hz a saccade under ~15° (≤54 ms) is one or two frames. Just command the new eye angle in one step and let the servo slew at full speed, with no easing. Main-sequence timing only matters for theatrical, deliberately slow glances; those should be eased (and are then "smooth pursuit-like" rather than saccades).
- Frank has no neck, so stay in the human eyes-only regime: keep routine gaze shifts within ±15-20° of centre, and save larger eye excursions for theatrical "side-eye" glances that are held briefly. With no VOR to model, the illusion relies on fast eyes plus a lagging "body" cue, for example lids and brows following 50-150 ms later as overlapping action.
- Engage mode (person detected): triangle saccades between the target's left eye, right eye and mouth/nose, with a uniform 0.1-0.5 s dwell. This also hides Frank's mechanically linked, non-verging eyes.
- Idle: look-around fixations of 0.5-3 s (an inference; see Gaps) with microsaccades of ~0.3-0.5° at ~1 Hz. On hobby servos 0.5° may be below the deadband, so use a 1° minimum or skip microsaccades if they cause chatter.
- Thinking state: on entering Thinking (the LLM is computing), schedule an upward cognitive aversion starting ~1.3 s before the predicted answer onset and lasting ~3.5 s (Gaussian), returning ~2.2 s after the answer starts. Because LLM latency is unknown in advance, trigger the aversion at the thinking onset and return to mutual gaze ~2.4 s before the end of each reply's final sentence.
- Speaking: an intimacy (side, small ×0.4 amplitude) aversion every ~4.75 s for ~2 s. Listening: aversions every ~7.2 s for ~1.1 s; listeners look at the speaker more.
- Lids follow vertical gaze: map the upper-lid target to eye pitch (lid ≈ a fixed margin above the pupil). Let downward lid motion be slightly faster than upward. Lower lids can follow upward gaze weakly.

### Gaps
- Fixation duration distributions for conversational face-viewing were not found from a primary source (the common 200-300 ms figure is for reading and scene viewing and was not verified).
- The Eyes Alive talk/listen interval and magnitude distributions were not retrieved (the PDF host redirects).
- Andrist's direction percentages are in a figure that extracted out of order. Only the qualitative pattern (cognitive = up, others = side) is reliable.
- I found no lid-to-eye gain (degrees of lid per degree of eye), since Malbouisson reports millimetres.
- Disney's "20 Hz" saccade figure is ambiguous (bandwidth vs rate). The paper does not define it further.

## 4. Blink models: rates by activity, duration and shape, gaze-evoked, phrase-boundary and reflexive blinks

### Takeaway
Spontaneous blinks are asymmetric, with a fast close and a slower, asymptotic open. Fully closing blinks of ~300 ms (9 frames at 30 fps) were rated most natural on a realistic face (~233 ms on a cartoon face), and data-driven curves beat both linear and ease-in/out curves. Mean rates are ~17/min at rest, ~26/min in conversation and ~4.5/min while reading or concentrating, and speaking raises the rate. Large gaze shifts almost always carry a blink. Listeners blink at speakers' pauses, and a long listener blink (~600 ms) works as a "got it, move on" signal.

### Cited Findings
- Blink rates (Bentivoglio et al. 1997, 150 healthy subjects): about 17 blinks/min at rest, 26 during conversation and 4.5 while reading. Conversation > rest > reading in 67.3% of subjects. Rate is "more influenced by cognitive processes than by age, eye color, or local factors" — [PubMed 9399231](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9399231/)
- Speaking raises the blink rate mainly through the motor act of talking, and auditory input alone did not change it (PLoS ONE 2021) — [PMC8500445](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500445/). The speaker blinks more when handling speech, and the listener blinks more only when taking in useful content (J Nonverbal Behav 2022) — [abstract](https://www.aminer.org/pub/628d22065aee126c0f441362/blinking-while-speaking-and-talking-hearing-and-listening-communication-or-individual-underlying)
- Trutoiu, Carter, Matthews & Hodgins, "Modeling and animating eye blinks" (ACM TAP 8(3), 2011), from 300 fps video ([Trutoiu et al. 2011](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Modeling-and-Animating-Eye-Blinks-Paper.pdf)):
  - "Individual human eye blinks are characterized by a fast eyelid closing and a slower, asymptotically converging eyelid opening." The down phase "is short in duration and achieves a high velocity with fast accelerations. The up phase lasts longer and decelerates more slowly."
  - The end of a blink can be defined as the moment the lid reaches 95% of the original opening (VanderWerf 2003).
  - Three actors averaged 8.2, 27.0 and 6.6 blinks/min.
  - Blink types are spontaneous, reflexive and voluntary, distinguishable "based on duration, amplitude, and context".
- Trutoiu et al. perception results, from the same paper ([PDF](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Modeling-and-Animating-Eye-Blinks-Paper.pdf)):
  - Blinks from the human-data model with fully closing lids were rated more natural than all simple profiles.
  - Rank order: model > asymmetric ease-in/out > symmetric ease-in/out > asymmetric linear > symmetric linear.
  - Durations tested were 7, 9, 11 and 13 frames at 30 fps. The best was 9 frames (≈300 ms) on the photorealistic character, which was also the dominant duration in the tracked data. The cartoon character preferred 7 frames (≈233 ms).
  - How the lower lid was animated made no difference.
  - Textbook "alert" blinks (Maestri) reverse the human dynamics.
- Blink states carry meaning: longer closure and reopening go with drowsiness, rate rises with some cognitive tasks (mental arithmetic), and elevated rates appear in people masking their emotions — [Trutoiu et al. 2011 (related-work section)](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Modeling-and-Animating-Eye-Blinks-Paper.pdf)
- Gaze-evoked blinks: orbicularis oculi EMG occurred with 97% of saccadic gaze shifts larger than 33°. Blinks "typically began simultaneously with the initiation of head and/or eye movement". With the eyes closed, EMG accompanied all head turns over 17°, starting on average 39.3 ms before head movement. "One component of the command for large saccadic gaze shifts appears to be a blink" (Evinger et al. 1994) — [Exp Brain Res 100:337-344 record](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/not-looking-while-leaping-the-linkage-of-blinking-and-saccadic-ga/). Blink probability rises with gaze amplitude — [Gandhi 2011 (PMC2822689)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822689)
- Phrase-boundary entrainment: listeners blinked ~0.25-0.5 s after the speaker's blinks, but only for speaker blinks at the end of speech and in pauses, and not when the video was muted (Nakano & Kitazawa 2010, Exp Brain Res 205:577-581) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00221-010-2387-z)
- Communicative listener blinks: in VR, a virtual listener's blink was set to "short" (208 ms) or "long" (607 ms). Speakers gave shorter answers to long blinks without noticing them. Long blinks may signal "I've received enough information for current purposes". In their corpus, most listener blinks fell at typical points in the conversation, and long blinks came mainly during mutual gaze and together with nods (Hömke, Holler & Levinson 2018, PLOS ONE 13(12): e0208030) — [PMC6291193](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6291193/)
- Disney implements blinks inside the L1 "alive" layer and gives the lids a 10 Hz motion bandwidth — [Pan et al. 2020](https://www.autoevolution.com/pdf/news_attachements/disney-research-robot-can-hold-your-gaze-fuel-your-nightmares-151000.pdf)

### Inferences
- Blink shape on servos at 50 Hz: a ~300 ms blink (15 frames) with closing over ~3-5 frames (60-100 ms, ease-in so it accelerates) and opening over ~10-12 frames (200-240 ms, exponential/ease-out approach). At 25 Hz the close is only 2 frames, so command the closed position directly and let the servo slew. Always close fully (Trutoiu). Upper lids are the essential part, since lower-lid treatment did not change ratings. The close/open split is an inference from the qualitative asymmetry; see Gaps.
- Rate by state:
  - Idle/rest: ~17/min (mean inter-blink interval ~3.5 s).
  - Speaking: ~26/min or higher (~2.3 s).
  - Listening: in between, higher when "taking heed".
  - Thinking/concentrating: drop toward reading rates (4-10/min), with a burst on the return of gaze.
  - Draw intervals from a skewed distribution (for example a gamma or lognormal with a minimum refractory of ~1 s) instead of fixed periods, so no identical loops.
- Event-triggered blinks, on top of the scheduler (and resetting it):
  - Gaze-evoked: blink with probability rising with gaze-shift amplitude, near-certain for Frank's largest shifts, starting together with the eye move.
  - Speech: blink at phrase boundaries and pauses in Frank's own TTS (from punctuation or audio-energy gaps).
  - Listening: blink 0.25-0.5 s after the human's pause or turn end.
  - Acknowledgement: a ~600 ms "long blink", with a brow or head-equivalent nod, as a backchannel when the human finishes a point.
  - Reflexive: on a sudden loud sound or an Interrupted event, a fast hard blink plus brow flinch.
- Blinks can be asymmetric now and then (Trutoiu noticed occasional left/right asymmetries), but keep the two eyes highly correlated by default.

### Gaps
- Trutoiu's millisecond split between closing and opening phases is in figures that did not extract as text. Only the qualitative asymmetry and the total duration are confirmed. Evinger et al. 1991 ("Eyelid movements: mechanisms and normal data") holds the classic numbers but was not retrieved.
- I found no single study giving blinks/min for speaker vs listener in the same conversation.
- I found no published reflexive (startle) blink duration in this pass.

## 5. Speech-face coordination: brow beats and prosody, visemes vs amplitude, audiovisual timing, co-articulation, listening and backchannels

### Takeaway
Eyebrow raises are linked to F0 rises, though only in ~71% of cases, and they boost the perceived prominence of words that carry a pitch accent. Pitch is the stronger cue; brows mostly help when pitch is ambiguous, and misaligned brows slow listeners down. Audiovisual speech tolerates the mouth leading the sound far more than lagging it (fusion over roughly −30 to +170 ms with visual lead favoured). So a servo mouth should be scheduled early to cancel actuator latency. Jaw and lips are separable controls (JALI). Backchannel timing can be cued by a ~110-120 ms low-pitch region after ≥700 ms of speech.

### Cited Findings
- Cavé et al. (ICSLP 1996): rapid rising-falling eyebrow movements were associated with F0 rises "in only 71% of the cases" (10 subjects reading, answering yes/no questions and in dialogue) — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_1996/cave96_icslp.html). A later analysis by the same group argues that eyebrow movements more often act as a turn-taking signal than as pure prosody — [Guaïtella et al. 2009, Language and Speech (summary)](https://sonicfield.org/library/are-eyebrow-movements-linked-to-voice-variations-and-turn-taking-in-dialogue-an-)
- Krahmer, Ruttkay, Swerts & Wesselink (ICSLP 2002), talking-head experiments: listeners preferred eyebrow movement on the same word as the pitch accent. Both cues affected perceived focus, "though pitch had a much larger effect". Brows mattered mainly when pitch cues were ambiguous. Brow movement increased the prominence of the accented word and lowered the prominence of nearby unaccented words — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_2002/krahmer02_icslp.html)
- Incongruent audiovisual prominence (brow on a different word from the pitch accent) gave longer reaction times than congruent cues — [Krahmer & Swerts, Interspeech 2006](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2006/krahmer06_interspeech.pdf). Eyebrow flashes, head nods and beat gestures all boost perceived word prominence, but eyebrows have "relatively weak cue value" — [Swerts & Krahmer work, search summary](https://www.isca-archive.org/avsp_2007/swerts07_avsp.pdf)
- Audiovisual sync window: McGurk fusion held from about −30 ms to +170 ms of asynchrony and was more robust when the audio lagged, defining a ~200 ms asymmetric integration window (van Wassenhove, Grant & Poeppel, 43 listeners) — [USUHS record](https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/temporal-window-of-integration-in-auditory-visual-speech-percepti/). Synchrony judgements span roughly 50 ms audio lead to 200 ms visual lead. The often-cited "visual speech leads audio by 150 ms" holds only at utterance onsets; in running speech, natural asynchrony is about 20 ms audio lead to 70 ms audio lag (Schwartz & Savariaux 2014) — [PMC4117430](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4117430/)
- A secondary summary attributes to Conrey & Pisoni that the visual stream had to lag by an average of 258 ms, or lead by 131 ms, before the asynchrony was noticed. The original paper and its sign convention were not checked, so treat this as unverified — [search summary; see PMC4744562](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4744562)
- JALI (Edwards, Landreth, Fiume & Singh, SIGGRAPH 2016) models speech with two independent anatomical controls, Jaw and Lip, on top of visemes. It notes that phoneme-to-viseme mapping is many-valued (co-articulation) — [JALI project page](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~elf/jali.html). The follow-up VisemeNet predicts JALI viseme curves from audio with ~120 ms lag — [arXiv 1805.09488](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.09488v1.pdf)
- Backchannel cue: Ward & Tsukahara (J. Pragmatics 32(8), 2000) found that low pitch late in an utterance cues listener backchannels in English and Japanese. Ward's prediction rule:
  - Japanese: a region below the 28th-percentile pitch lasting ≥110 ms, after ≥700 ms of speech.
  - English: below the 23rd percentile for ≥120 ms, after ≥700 ms.
  — [Ward chapter draft](https://www.cs.utep.edu/nigel/papers/wilks-chapter); [paper abstract](https://www.cs.utep.edu/nigel/abstracts/jprag00.html)
- Listener long blinks co-occur with nods as feedback, and long blinks shorten the speaker's answer — [Hömke et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6291193/). Listener blinks entrain to the speaker's pause blinks 0.25-0.5 s later — [Nakano & Kitazawa 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00221-010-2387-z)
- iCat lip-synced while tracking a head, blinking and holding expressions at the same time, which a layered animation engine made possible — [van Breemen 2004 abstract](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2101506210)

### Inferences
- Brow beats from prosody: run a pitch tracker (or use TTS word timings plus stress marks) on Frank's own outgoing audio. On detected pitch-accent peaks, fire a brief additive inner+outer brow raise (an eyebrow flash): rise ~100-150 ms, hold ~100-200 ms, fall ~200-300 ms. These durations are inferences, not from the sources. Place the peak at or slightly before the F0 peak. Fire on at most one word per phrase (focus word), not every stressed syllable, since Cavé's 71% and Krahmer's "weak cue" both argue for sparing use. Never fire on a word that is not accented (incongruence costs listeners).
- AV latency budget: the mouth may lead the sound by up to ~100-150 ms with no penalty, but audio leading the mouth becomes noticeable at a few tens of ms. Measure end-to-end servo latency (Python → USB serial → Pico → PWM → mechanical slew; plausibly 40-120 ms for hobby servos) and run lip-sync analysis on a look-ahead buffer. Command jaw targets that much earlier than the audio frame they belong to, plus a small extra lead (~30-50 ms), which also acts as anticipation.
- Amplitude-driven jaw versus visemes: with 2 jaw + 4 lip + 4 corner servos, a JALI-style split fits. Jaw opening follows a smoothed loudness envelope (fast attack, ~60-100 ms release). The lip and corner channels add coarse viseme classes (closed lips for /p b m/ from silence-onset bursts, rounded for /o u/, wide for /i e/), even if only from spectral centroid or brightness. This extends the existing loudness→jaw and brightness→corners mapping. Co-articulation comes for free from the per-channel smoothing filter.
- Listening behaviour without identical loops:
  - Brow backchannel ("hmm" flash or soft knit) and/or a long blink + slight smile on Ward-style low-pitch cues in the human's speech, rate-limited (for example at most one per 3-5 s), with randomised variant and amplitude.
  - Intimacy aversions per Andrist's listening parameters (every ~7.2 s for ~1.1 s).
  - Mostly mutual gaze with triangle saccades.
  - Blink entrainment to the human's pauses.

### Gaps
- I found no robot-specific study of brow beats on a physical face, and no measured onset/apex/offset durations for eyebrow flashes; the numbers above are inferences.
- I found no published servo-lip-sync latency compensation figures for animatronics.
- The JALI paper's own co-articulation timing (anticipation in ms) was not retrieved.
- "Visemes vs amplitude" perception on a physical animatronic: no comparative study found.

## 6. Expression timing (onset/apex/offset; posed vs spontaneous; surprise) and how theatrical motion is perceived on robots vs the uncanny valley

### Takeaway
Ordinary ("macro") facial expressions last ~0.5-4 s. Spontaneous smile onsets take ~0.5-0.75 s, and deliberate smiles have faster, larger onsets and offsets than spontaneous ones. In genuine surprise the upper lids rise before the brows, while deliberate surprise moves them together, so staggering features reads as natural. Exaggeration increases engagement once it is clearly noticeable, but movement amplifies the uncanny valley when the face is near-human. Stylised, clearly theatrical motion that is always alive, with eased timing, is the safer target than near-realism.

### Cited Findings
- Expressions lasting 0.5-4 s are conventionally treated as macro-expressions (Ekman et al. 1980-1998; Matsumoto & Hwang 2011) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01293/full); [arXiv 2409.00017](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00017v1). Micro-expressions are defined as under 500 ms in total or under 260 ms of onset — [Yan et al. 2013, J Nonverbal Behav](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/s10919-013-0159-8)
- The typical phase sequence is neutral → onset → apex → offset → neutral; multiple apices also occur — [Micro-expression survey (arXiv 1806.05781)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05781)
- Smiles: spontaneous smiles had smaller amplitude and "a larger and more consistent relation between amplitude and duration than deliberate smiles". Timing and amplitude alone classified posed vs spontaneous smiles with 93% accuracy (Cohn & Schmidt 2004) — [CMU RI](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/the-timing-of-facial-motion-in-posed-and-spontaneous-smiles). Within subjects, "onset and offset speed, amplitude of movement, and offset duration were greater in deliberate smiles" (Schmidt, Ambadar, Cohn & Reed 2006) — [abstract](https://sites.pitt.edu/~kschmidt/schmidt06DSabstract.htm). Spontaneous smile onsets average ~0.5-0.75 s, and solitary spontaneous smiles ~0.52 s — [review via PMC2668537 (search summary)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2668537). Onset duration was independent of social context, and maximum velocity scaled consistently with amplitude (Schmidt, Cohn & Tian 2003) — [abstract](https://sites.pitt.edu/~kschmidt/schmidt03abstract.htm)
- Surprise (Namba, Matsui & Zloteanu 2021, Sci Rep):
  - All displays were mainly brow-raise (AU1+2) plus upper-lid raise (AU5).
  - Mimicked ("external") deliberate displays had faster brow and lid movement.
  - Earlier work found that genuine surprise raises the upper lids (AU5) before the brows, while deliberate surprise moves both at once.
  - Genuine displays had the weakest coupling between movements and included horizontal movement on the left of the face.
  — [PMC7873236](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7873236)
- On EMYS, making surprise "more instant" (with exaggerated eye-popping) helped separate it from joy — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2012](https://priceless-mirzakhani-65130a.netlify.app/publication/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life/ribeiro-2012-hri-illusion-of-robotic-life.pdf)
- Exaggerated motion on a robot raised engagement, entertainment value, predictability of partner gaze and recall. Its levels map onto robot-like, human-like and cartoon-like expectations, and it only registers above a threshold — [Gielniak & Thomaz 2012](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/social-machines/papers/gielniak12_hri_exaggeration.pdf)
- Mori (2012 authorised translation): "Movement changes the shape of the uncanny valley graph by amplifying the peaks and valleys", with movement whose speed and acceleration approximate human movement. A still corpse sits in the valley and zombies (moving) at its deepest point. Mori meant the valley as design advice — [IEEE Spectrum translation](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-uncanny-valley)
- A motionless head "loses its sense of liveliness", which motivated always-on Perlin idle — [Andrist et al. 2014](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~bilge/pubs/2014/HRI14-Andrist.pdf). Disney "just wants it to look right", aiming for visual appeal over neuro-faithful modelling, and the press noted their bust sits in potential uncanny territory — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/disney-research-robotic-gaze)
- Appearance shapes what motion feels natural: on a cartoon character viewers preferred shorter blinks (7 frames) than on a photorealistic one (9 frames) — [Trutoiu et al. 2011](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Modeling-and-Animating-Eye-Blinks-Paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- Default expression envelope for the emotion animator:
  - Onset 400-750 ms for "sincere" changes (smile, sadness), 100-250 ms for surprise and startle.
  - Apex hold driven by the synthesiser.
  - Offset slower than onset for spontaneous-reading emotions (Schmidt: deliberate smiles have faster offsets).
  - A full one-shot expression should sit in the 0.5-4 s macro range. Anything much shorter reads as a micro-expression or a twitch.
- "Theatrical but natural": on a Frankenstein character, use larger amplitude (exaggeration gain) while keeping spontaneous-style timing (eased onsets, amplitude-proportional speed, slower offsets), so it reads as big rather than fake. Deliberate-looking cues (simultaneous onset of every feature, fast symmetric offsets) are what make expressions look posed.
- Stagger features: eyes (0 ms) → upper lids (+0-50 ms) → brows (+50-150 ms) → mouth/corners (+100-300 ms), with small random jitter and per-side asymmetry (one brow or corner leads by 30-80 ms). This follows Namba's genuine-surprise ordering, Disney's eyes-first overlapping action and the character bible's "distinct timing". The exact offsets are design choices, not published values.
- Uncanny risk: Frank is a stylised monster head with silicone skin, so it sits near the valley. Following Mori, motion that is nearly but not quite human (jerky servo stepping, frozen stillness, all features moving in lockstep) will hurt most. Continuous L1 life, smoothing filters that hide servo steps, and frank stylisation (clear theatrical poses held briefly) are the mitigations.

### Gaps
- I found no published onset/apex/offset durations for anger, fear, disgust or sadness from FACS timing studies. Hess & Kleck (1990/1994) were not retrieved.
- Namba et al.'s actual millisecond lags between AU5 and AU1+2 were not extracted.
- I found no study that directly measured theatrical vs realistic facial timing on a physical robot face against uncanny ratings. The Gielniak result is for arm and body motion.
