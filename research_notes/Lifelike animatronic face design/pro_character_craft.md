# Professional character-animatronic craft, and what it means for a Frankenstein's-monster head

Source labels used below: **[P]** = first-hand practitioner statement (direct quote or practitioner-authored text); **[J]** = journalist's paraphrase of practitioners or of a press release; **[F]** = fan site, blog, student work, retail listing, fan wiki or other secondary source. **(snippet)** = taken from a search-result summary, because the full page could not be fetched (several fetches returned HTTP 403/402/500). Treat (snippet) items as leads to verify, not as checked quotes. All dates are publication dates. Research done 2026-10-30.

## 1. Disney Imagineering: how they approach facial performance, and what they say makes a figure feel alive

### Takeaway
Imagineering's stated aim is figures that act like "actors" in "the grand tradition of Disney animation", built by people trained as animators using tools that carry "the artist's hand" straight onto the hardware. When Imagineers explain what makes a face come alive, they point to tiny secondary motions (cheek twitches, eyelid flickers), to eyebrows, to a specific signature trait (Walt's eye glint), and, for stylised characters, to keeping the cartoon's non-physical motion instead of correcting it toward realism.

**Animation-first pipelines and stylised figures**
- [P] "Pixels to Parks" (Gene Lee, Aaron Adams, Evan Goldberg, Justin Walker; SIGGRAPH 2013 talk), a WDI and Walt Disney Animation Studios pipeline for the Lumiere figure (Enchanted Tales with Belle), was meant to be "flexible and intuitive for feature film animators". - [J] (snippet) For Olaf, Imagineers worked with Walt Disney Animation Studios and studied the original animation frame by frame for "accuracy in movement and personality". The mouth uses a single actuator that drives both upper and lower jaw together. The eyes have independent yaw, with pitch and blink driven remotely by a four-bar linkage. Josh Gad pre-recorded a large number of lines and jokes. The nose, arms, buttons, brows and hair attach magnetically for gags (per Kyle Laughlin, SVP R&D, WDI). — [Attractions Magazine](https://attractionsmagazine.com/how-imagineers-brought-a-free-roaming-robotic-olaf-to-life/); [WDWInfo](https://www.wdwinfo.com/news-stories/disney-unveils-next-generation-robotic-olaf-at-disneyland-paris/)

- [F] (snippet) Davy Feiten started as an animator and later programmed EPCOT's American Adventure, World of Motion and Universe of Energy figures. — [Tomorrow Society podcast](https://tomorrowsociety.com/davy-feiten-podcast/)
- [F] (snippet) WDI's job title for the role is "Show Programmer, Figure Animator". A current listing includes building rigid-body-dynamics models "to optimize figure movement and performance". — [Disney Careers listing](https://emplois.disneycareers.com/emploi/glendale/principal-show-programmer-figure-animator/391/87515800208)
- [F] Park Lore (Jul 2025) argues that Marc Davis's Pirates of the Caribbean figures "border on caricature", which kept them out of the uncanny valley, and that stylised figures stayed the norm (Haunted Mansion, Country Bear Jamboree, Jungle Cruise). Its claim that Frozen Ever After decided "We won't build a face at all" (projected faces) is the author's own framing, not a Disney statement. ### Inferences
- Disney's language treats the figure as an actor performing animation, not as a robot playing back positions. The people who program faces are hired as animators ("Figure Animator"), and the tools are judged by how directly they carry an animator's intent onto hardware. For Frank, the expressive layer should be authored as animation (curves and poses designed by eye) on top of the LLM-driven runtime, never as raw servo targets.
- The two motions Rohde names (cheek and eyelid micro-motion) and the two Fitzgerald names (eyebrows and eye glint) are cheap on this head: EyeMech lids, the two MG90S brows, and eye shading or catchlights. The Shaman's 40 face functions are not needed to get the effect Rohde describes. What matters is that small, independent, low-amplitude motions keep happening.
- Hondo is held up as a showpiece with a reported ~10 face functions, while the Shaman has ~40. Jaw, lips, 2-axis eyes, lids and two brows put this head roughly in Hondo's range for facial channels. That is enough to be considered "expressive" by park standards if the animation is good. The function counts are journalists' figures, so this is an order-of-magnitude comparison only.
- Olaf (a single jaw actuator; eyes with yaw, pitch and blink) is evidence that Disney's 2025 stylised showpiece uses a simple face and puts the effort into motion quality, personality and pre-recorded gags.
- Hughes's point that costume motion absorbs mechanical stiffness suggests that Frank's fringe of hair (part of the Pierce look, see section 5), and any loose collar or bolt hardware that can swing slightly, will add free, physically real secondary motion that hides servo stepping.

## 2. Garner Holt, Henson, Stan Winston/Legacy, Spectral Motion, Animatronic Concepts, Universal Creative: face design, DOF, and live vs programmed vs hybrid control

### Takeaway
Garner Holt Productions bets on a high function count plus a skin engineered to wrinkle and to attach compliantly to the mechanism, with "the skin is the real star" as its creed. Henson's philosophy runs the other way: a character feels alive because a live performer, usually the one doing the voice, drives the face in real time and can react and improvise. Recording and editing that performance (the Henson Performance Editor) is the bridge to repeatable shows. No usable first-hand material was found on face design at Legacy Effects, Spectral Motion, Animatronic Concepts or Universal Creative.

### Cited Findings
**Garner Holt Productions (GHP)**
- [P] InPark Magazine's GHP feature (written by Bill Butler, GHP Creative Design Director, c. Nov 2017) says the Lincoln bust has 45 facial functions. The Yeti has 130+ motions, over 30 of them in the face, driven mostly by linear pneumatics. The "Expressive Head" fits almost twice the Yeti's face functions into a smaller frame. GHP uses custom servo motors, unlike the hobby servos common in film and university heads. The silicone skin is held to the mechanism by many flexible magnetic grippers, a balance between hold strength and compliance, and can be swapped in under two minutes without tools. It is designed to form lifelike wrinkles and creases. — [InPark Magazine](https://www.inparkmagazine.com/its-a-dimensional-thing-garner-holt/amp/)
- [P] Garner Holt in the same piece: expression is "an effective way to illustrate an animatronic character's scripted intentions" (signalling friendly or hostile intent). The expressions (scowl, raised brows, smile, frown) are "all accomplished mechanically beneath the animatronics' silicone face masks." — [InPark Magazine](https://www.inparkmagazine.com/its-a-dimensional-thing-garner-holt/amp/)
- [P] Bob Gurr, Disney Legend: "I'm still flabbergasted with what I saw the Lincoln face do." — [InPark Magazine](https://www.inparkmagazine.com/its-a-dimensional-thing-garner-holt/amp/)
- [J] (snippet) Holt said the Lincoln head's expressions can look exaggerated because the robot is "designed to show off just how much expression can be exhibited". — [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/?p=1225899)

### Inferences
- The project already sits on the hybrid model the pros converged on. The Henson rule (live performance, performer = voice) maps onto an LLM that both speaks and drives the face in real time. The Henson Performance Editor model (record a live take, clean it, play it back) maps onto canned, hand-polished "signature bits" that the runtime can trigger. The pro lesson: let the speaking agent own the face's timing, so the face reacts to what is being said instead of running a separate idle loop that ignores the speech.
- GHP's magnetic-gripper skin attachment and the 2017 Trump critique (section 4) both say the skin-to-mechanism coupling is as important as the actuators. Dragon Skin snap pads should hold the skin close to the underskull at the moving points (brows, lid margins, mouth corners) so the skin moves with the mechanism instead of tenting or lagging.
- GHP's Lincoln "exaggerated to show range" and Olaf's single jaw actuator mark the two ends of the spectrum. A fun Frankenstein should borrow GHP's willingness to push expressions past natural range, not its function count.

## 3. Show programming practice: keyframe vs captured performance, cited principles, and avoiding mouth-flapping, dead eyes and symmetry

### Takeaway
Pros program faces the way animators animate: performance is designed by eye (on a virtual rig, or performed live on a waldo and then edited), then pushed through hardware limits. On lip-sync, puppeteers' rules are concrete: one mouth opening per syllable, open on the sound so words are not "eaten", drive the lower jaw more than the top, few holds, and do the lip-sync first, then layer the emotion over it. Little first-hand Imagineering text on overshoot, settle or eye leads was findable online. That material lives in talks and books, and in the earlier research round.

### Cited Findings
- [P] Disney's Lumiere pipeline: animators choreograph on a virtual rig, software flags physical constraints, artists rework the motion to be physically plausible, then transfer it. — [Pixels to Parks, SIGGRAPH 2013](https://disneyanimation.com/publications/pixels-to-parks-new-animation-techniques-for-fantasyland/)
- [P] Hondo tools let animators "set joint speeds and pivots" while moving 3D data between design and animation software (Victoria Thomas). — [BlogMickey, Feb 2019](https://blogmickey.com/2019/02/photos-video-hondo-audio-animatronic-figure-behind-the-scenes-testing-list-of-aas-coming-to-star-wars-galaxys-edge/)
- [F] (snippet) Alfredo Ayala's contribution was programming "without the use of complicated sliders and buttons, allowing more intuitive, natural movement". — [TEA Connect](https://www.teaconnect.org/masters/alfredo-ayala)
- [P] Henson Performance Editor (from 1990): record a live puppeteer take, edit it, play it back. — via [Muppet Wiki](https://muppet.fandom.com/wiki/Henson_Performance_Control_System) (fan wiki)
- [F] (snippet) Puppetry lip-sync rule: the mouth opens once per syllable, rather than flapping continuously. — [Puppet Nerd, "Puppetry 101"](https://puppetnerd.com/puppetry-101/); [WonderHowTo](https://puppetry-arts.wonderhowto.com/how-to/make-puppet-lip-synch-309219)
- [P] (snippet) Steve Axtell (professional puppet builder) advises opening the mouth on the sounds so the words don't get "eaten", and moving the puppet's lower jaw more than the top. — [Axtell Expressions](https://axtell.com/?p=11238)
- [F] (snippet) Lip-sync technique has to change with the speed and rhythm of the dialogue. Puppet Nerd also lists a "Henson Punch" among lip-sync considerations, but the summary did not define it. — [Puppet Nerd](https://puppetnerd.com/puppetry-101/)
- [F] (snippet) A puppetry training guide: "Do not be distracted if your lip sync is off… Refocus and keep going", and use puppet "vibrato" sparingly ("do not abuse it"). — [Guide to Puppetry (Scribd)](https://www.scribd.com/document/550626417/guide-to-puppetry-20-1-6c)
- [P] (snippet) CG facial-animation practice (Inspired 3D, AWN): "Animate the lip-sync first; then go in behind it and modify the face to fit the emotion." Lip-sync "needs to have flow from one sound to the next, with relatively few holds." — [AWN VFXWorld](https://www.awn.com/print/vfxworld/inspired-3d-lip-sync-and-facial-animation-part-1)
- [F] (snippet) Imagineer Darrin Hughes: costume motion absorbs some of the figure's non-natural motion. — [Jim Hill Media](https://jimhillmedia.com/marvelous-mechanized-magic-kingdom-event-dispelled-many-myths-about-audio-animatronics/?amp=1)
- [J] On Olaf, the team embraced the animation's non-physical motion instead of correcting it toward physics. — [WDWNT, Dec 2025](https://wdwnt.com/2025/12/disney-research-hub-shares-technical-details-behind-new-olaf-animatronic/)

### Inferences
- Concrete anti-flapping rules for Frank's mouth driver: (1) one jaw open per syllable or vowel nucleus, timed slightly ahead of the audio onset, never a jaw that tracks raw amplitude sample by sample; (2) jaw does most of the work, lips shape; (3) bigger opens on stressed syllables and smaller ones on unstressed, so the mouth follows the sentence's rhythm; (4) emotion is a separate layer (brows, lids, mouth-corner bias) over the sync, as in the CG workflow; (5) few dead holds inside a phrase, but a real close at phrase ends.
- "Monster" speech gives licence to exaggerate. Karloff's later monster grunts and Boyle's "Puttin' on the Ritz" grunt-singing (section 5) suggest slightly over-wide, slow jaw opens on stressed words read as in character instead of as a glitch.
- Live-then-clean (Henson) suggests a practical authoring tool for this project: record Lars puppeteering the head from a gamepad or slider "waldo" to a sound clip, then edit the curves. That gives captured spontaneity for canned bits, with the LLM runtime covering the rest.
- Dead eyes and symmetry: the pros' remedies found here are indirect (micro-motion per Rohde; Walt's glint; del Toro's asymmetric eyes in section 5). The earlier research round covers gaze and blink timing.

### Gaps
- No first-hand Imagineering or Henson statements found online on anticipation, overshoot, settle, holds or eyes-lead-head as programmed on figures. Likely sources not reached: SIGGRAPH/GDC talks by WDI figure animators, *The Imagineering Field Guide* books, *The Illusion of Life*, Stan Winston School course videos. The 2013 Lumiere write-up PDF ("LumiereWriteup.pdf", linked from the Disney Animation page) was not read.
- The definition of the "Henson Punch" was not found.

## 4. Lessons from failures, and the uncanny valley as practitioners discuss it

### Takeaway
The famous failures fail in the face, not the body: skin that doesn't track the underskull, a mouth that doesn't move the skin, eyes that diverge or don't focus, and an off-model likeness. Excellent body programming does not rescue a bad face. Practitioners and critics treat stylisation and caricature as the escape route from the uncanny valley. Research points to abnormal eyes as the trigger, so a monster face can be grotesque without being uncanny if its eyes look focused, alive and engaged.

### Cited Findings
- [J] Disney's Trump figure (Hall of Presidents, Dec 2017) was widely mocked: it was said to look like Jon Voight or Hillary Clinton. — [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/travel/disney-world-slammed-for-horrible-animatronic-trump-that-looks-like-jon-voight); [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/articles/354791/animatronic-trump-hillary-clinton/)
- [J/F] An unnamed animatronics expert consulted by MiceChat (28 Dec 2017) blamed the face. The skin "doesn't seem to be adhering properly to the underskull", which distorts the silicone. The mouth movements did not move the skin convincingly, and functions that would sell the illusion seemed to be missing. Only the bottom teeth showed, over a weak jaw and chin. The eyes "diverged" and did not appear focused. The colour was too pale. Yet "The body and arms move very well and are programmed exceptionally well." — [MiceChat](https://www.micechat.com/181457-awkward-animatronic-donald-trump-joins-hall-presidents/)
- [J] Disney gave the figure a new face in a 2025 refurbishment. — [WDWNT, Jun 2025](https://wdwnt.com/2025/06/disney-updates-donald-trump-animatronic-for-the-hall-of-presidents-following-hillary-clinton-controversy/)
- [F] Park Lore (Jul 2025) on the 2025 Walt figure: close-up footage suggests the mouth "operates in 1s and 0s" rather than convincingly lip-syncing. Some of Walt's family voiced discomfort. Former Imagineer Eddie Sotto tweeted (14 Jul 2025): "I wonder if it's even possible for an AA to emulate the charisma." The same essay cites the 2011–12 Little Mermaid Ariel figures (oversized doll eyes, a mouth stretching "pupil to pupil") as sliding toward the valley. — [Park Lore](https://parklore.com/vault/uncanny-valley/)
- [J] (snippet) Sophia (Hanson Robotics): Yann LeCun called it a "puppet", and Quartz's experts called it "a chatbot with a face". Critics put its face in the uncanny valley as "too close to human without being human". David Hanson has argued that cartoonish features may help. — [Hanson Robotics (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanson_Robotics); [Personified Systems](https://medium.com/personified-systems/sophia-uncanny-and-untrustworthy-4471da0c1795); [Singularity Hub, Feb 2018](https://singularityhub.com/2018/02/18/what-roboticists-are-learning-from-the-first-generation-of-lifelike-humanoid-robots/)
- [P] (snippet) Seyama & Nagayama (Presence, 2007): the most unpleasant impressions came from highly realistic faces, but the valley appeared only when the realistic faces had abnormal features such as bizarre eyes. Realism alone was necessary but not sufficient. — [The Uncanny Valley: Effect of Realism on the Impression of Artificial Human Faces](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1162/pres.16.4.337)
- [J] (snippet) Disney Research (2012, physical face cloning): the face "has always been, and still is, the most difficult and labour-intensive part" of realistic animatronic characters. — [Science Illustrated](https://scienceillustrated.com.au/blog/science/disney-takes-face-cloning-to-an-impressive-new-level/)
- [P] (snippet) Brian Henson: animatronics are "one notch more believable" than hand puppets. — [UPI, Oct 2025](https://www.upi.com/Entertainment_News/Movies/2025/10/10/Brian-Henson-says-Dark-Crystal-re-release-shows-animatronic-origins/9141760028571/)

### Inferences
- Failure checklist for this head, ordered by how often it kills figures: (1) skin must follow the underskull at the moving zones (snap pads close to brow, lid and mouth hardware; no air gaps); (2) the mouth must visibly move skin around it (cheeks and mouth corners, not just the jaw hinge); (3) eyes must converge on a real target and never drift apart; (4) the overall sculpt must read "Frankenstein" in silhouette before anything moves.
- A Frankenstein's monster is an advantage here. Nobody expects it to pass as a real human, so the face sits on the stylised side of the valley by default. The risk comes back if the eyes look wrong: glassy, unfocused, diverging, or with no catchlight. The design should spend its realism budget on the eyes (wet-looking iris, catchlight like Walt's glint, good convergence) and caricature everything else.
- Sophia shows the conversational trap. If the LLM talks fluently while the face gives little back, viewers call it a "chatbot with a face". The face has to react to the content (listening brows, thinking gaze-aversion, delighted lid-widening), or the conversation feels pasted on.

### Gaps
- The original 1964 Lincoln's problems, the 1971 Hall of Presidents and other early-figure issues were not documented by practitioners in the sources found.
- No named-practitioner discussion of the uncanny valley (e.g. a WDI interview) was found. The caricature argument is from a fan essay plus academic work.

## 5. The character: Pierce/Karloff 1931 and later interpretations; defining features and motions; fun vs scary

### Takeaway
The 1931 design is a dead face with live eyes. Pierce built a flat-topped, high square skull, a heavy brow, wax-heavy lids that deaden the gaze, a sunken cheek, thin dead lips, a scar and neck electrodes. Karloff then played an "innocent" through that mask, using the eyes and hands. Every sympathetic or funny version (Bride, Young Frankenstein, Hotel Transylvania, del Toro 2025) keeps the iconic silhouette and turns up the innocence, wonder and pathos. A fun Frank should keep Pierce's shapes and rest pose, then break them with very alive, childlike eyes and brows.

### Cited Findings
**Jack Pierce's 1931 makeup**
- [J] Pierce built up Karloff's forehead with cotton and spirit gum sealed with collodion, a high square forehead with fringe bangs. Green greasepaint gave a deadish cast on black-and-white film. Scars, stitches and hair were painted and glued on. Silver electrodes (not bolts) were glued to the neck. Heavy eyelids were made of mortician's wax after Karloff said his eyes "look too alive." Karloff's dental bridge was removed to sink his right cheek. Other features: sunken jaw, jutting chin, thin dead lips, blackened fingertips, a stiff face, long grasping fingers. Under the heavy lids, something "very much conscious and alive." Application took 3–3.5 hours each morning. — [Jamie Zvirzdin, "The Makeup of a Monster", Creative Nonfiction, Issue 66](https://creativenonfiction.org/writing/the-makeup-of-a-monster/)
- [J] Conflicting credit for the lids: some accounts say they were Karloff's idea ("eyes look too alive") and others credit Pierce's own "flat-topped, heavy-lidded" concept. — [Creative Nonfiction](https://creativenonfiction.org/writing/the-makeup-of-a-monster/) vs [TCM: Jack Pierce](https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/person/152336%7C76063/Jack-Pierce)
- [F] (snippet) Pierce's rationale for the flat head was that a surgeon opening the skull would leave it squared off like a box ("shoebox"), with a scar and metal clamps. Another account says it came from morgue battery jars. The flatness was helped by a wig with a cotton roll on top. — [Spooky Isles](https://www.spookyisles.com/jack-pierce-frankenstein-makeup/); [Movie Fanfare](https://www.moviefanfare.com/?p=53008)

**Karloff's performance**
- [P] Karloff: "Whale and I both saw the character as an innocent one. Within the heavy restrictions of my makeup, I tried to play it that way." Also: "This was a pathetic creature who, like us all, had neither wish nor say in his creation," and on fan mail: "I found all my letters heavy with it" (sympathy). — [MeTV, 15 Oct 2025](https://metv.com/stories/boris-karlof-had-a-theory-about-why-audiences-loved-his-monster) (original interview or book not named)
- [F] (snippet) Because the 1931 monster had no dialogue, its sympathy rested on face and movement. The BFI says Pierce's makeup does not bury the performance: watch his eyes and hands. — [The Artifice](https://the-artifice.com/boris-karloff-monster/); [BFI: Boris Karloff, 10 essential films](https://www.bfi.org.uk/lists/boris-karloff-10-essential-films)
- [F] (snippet) Karloff opposed having the monster speak in *Bride of Frankenstein* (1935), believing a speaking monster would lose the audience's sympathy. — [Zombo's Closet](https://www.zomboscloset.com/?p=3170)

**Later interpretations**
- [J] *Young Frankenstein* (1974): Peter Boyle's monster is "an almost entirely physical performance, but his facial expressions–even exaggerated–are ideal." — [The Stop Button, 2010](https://thestopbutton.com/2010/07/07/young-frankenstein-1974-mel-brooks/). [J] TIME calls him "the funniest of all the screen Frankenstein monsters". — [TIME](https://entertainment.time.com/2014/01/28/frankenstein-monsters-list/slide/peter-boyle/). [F] The signature gag is the monster in top hat and tails, grunt-singing "Puttin' on the Ritz". — [Wikipedia: Young Frankenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Frankenstein)
- [J] (snippet) *Hotel Transylvania* (2012): the filmmakers told Empire, "We wanted to make Frankenstein feel fresh and new while retaining the iconic elements that make him such a recognisable character." — [Empire](https://empireonline.com/movies/features/hotel-transylvania-characters). [J] (snippet) Sony rebuilt rigs and the pipeline for a year to get Tartakovsky's 2D-style squash and stretch in CG. — [AnimationWorld](https://d1xfgk3mh635yx.cloudfront.net/animationworld/hotel-transylvania-gets-2d-transfusion). [F] (snippet) Student analyses note that Frank is drawn blocky and angular (shape language). — [Ulster student blog](https://blogs.ulster.ac.uk/b00841575-bdes-animation/2022/12/23/genndy-tartakovsky-and-hotel-transylvania)
- [P] *Frankenstein* (Guillermo del Toro, Netflix, 2025), creature designed by Mike Hill. Hill: "We agreed that we didn't want all these garish wounds and stitching." He described the eyes as deliberately unequal: "When he wants to display his more monstrous side, we could focus on the big eye… When he's leaning into his more human side, we shoot him from the right eye, which is the smaller eye." Hill says Elordi had "very doleful brown eyes that were a lot like Boris Karloff's eyes", that they "intensified" his cheekbones, that there was "almost this elegance to him and his mannerisms", and that he starts as "basically a nubile newborn". Elordi: "It's designed so that you focus on the Creature's eyes, which is where his soul is… His eyes are where the human is." Also: "all the scars and the brow point toward my expression". Del Toro's references were mostly 1920s–30s silent films, so the job was to "understand that nuance of expression". — [Netflix Tudum, 25 Feb 2026](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/features/jacob-elordi-mike-hill-frankenstein)
- [J] (snippet) The del Toro creature used 42 prosthetic pieces (14 on the head and neck), and Hill wanted it to look as if it came from the 1800s, not the modern day. — [Variety AU](https://au.variety.com/2025/more/news/frankenstein-jacob-elordi-prosthetics-guillermo-del-toro-29072/). Makeup sessions ran up to 10 hours. — [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-features/making-of-guillermo-del-toro-frankenstein-creature-1236438374/). Hill wanted a "less garish" creature that did not look like an accident victim. — [KTVZ (wire story)](https://ktvz.com/?p=1576494)

### Inferences
- **Defining static features** (must read in silhouette, before anything moves): flat-topped high square cranium with a fringe of bangs, heavy overhanging brow, heavy upper lids, hollow cheeks (Karloff's was asymmetric: the right cheek), thin lips, long jaw and jutting chin, forehead scar or staples, neck electrodes. These belong to the skull and skin sculpt, not the mechanism.
- **Defining motions**: slow, deliberate, slightly stiff head and neck; heavy-lidded resting gaze; eyes and brows that carry the inner life (Karloff's "innocent", Elordi's "where his soul is"); a gaping, effortful mouth when vocalising.
- **The core performance trick to borrow**: a "dead" mask (stiff face, heavy lids at rest) against "very much conscious and alive" eyes. On this head, park the lids low (heavy, Pierce-style) as the neutral pose, and make every expressive beat open them. Wonder, delight, surprise and attention become large lid and brow changes from a heavy baseline. That gives the biggest readable contrast for the smallest servo travel.
- **Fun vs scary**: the scary version keeps the lids heavy, the brows down, slow, and adds sudden fast moves (Universal's "fast-moving" monsters). The fun version keeps the same shapes but behaves like Karloff's innocent, del Toro's "newborn" or Boyle's showman: childlike brow raises, wide-eyed curiosity, delighted double takes, slow comic "processing" holds, big exaggerated reactions (Boyle's exaggerated faces, Hotel Transylvania's squash-and-stretch spirit). Because Frank talks (the thing Karloff feared would cost sympathy), the face has to supply the innocence: earnest, slightly slow, wondering.
- **Asymmetry is canon**: Karloff's single sunken cheek and del Toro's deliberately unequal eyes both support a slightly asymmetric neutral (one brow or lid a touch lower). That kills robotic mirror-symmetry and is on-model.
- **Speaking style**: the classic monster's speech is effortful and grunting. Slightly over-wide, slow jaw opens on stressed syllables are in character and fun.

### Gaps

- No Tartakovsky or Sony animator first-hand quote specifically about animating Frank's face was found. Student blogs are not reliable for this.

### Inferences
- Consumer props reduce the monster to a mouth flap plus glowing eyes. Glowing eyes trade "alive" for "spooky", the opposite of the Karloff and del Toro principle that the eyes are where the soul is. Frank should keep realistic, focused eyes and skip LED-glow eyes, at least in "fun" mode.
- Universal's 2025 monsters are tuned for scares (speed, power, scale). That is the reference for what not to do in the fun mode, and a useful contrast pose set ("lunge", fast head snap) for an occasional mock-scare gag.
